Jian turned the miniature arteries between forefinger and thumb, the pulse ticking against his glove like a miniature metronome. “Next time you decide to gift-wrap capillaries, tell me first. I’ve only got two pockets.”

Gideon’s laugh came out a half-cough, tasting of copper where the barrier had kissed his lungs. “Gift? You’re the one who insisted on souvenirs.”

“Souvenir implies choice,” Jian muttered, but the corner of his mouth softened. “Protocol says we tag any tissue that retains autonomous rhythm. If it syncs above ninety bpm outside the host, I file an Incident.”

Corv drifted closer, ribbon of membrane trailing across Sona’s bootlaces. “Autonomous rhythm,” he echoed, dry as old paper. “You mean the heart learned the chorus faster than we did.”

Sona flexed her throat—still raw from the final note—before answering. “It wasn’t the heart. It was the entire layer deciding whether to keep us.” She looked at Jian. “You want to file, file. Just tell them the pulse came with a watermark.”

She lifted her wrist; faint bruises in the shape of a reversed crescent showed against the skin, evidence of the breach’s bite. “I signed in blood,” she added, voice too flat to be dramatic. “They can match the font.”

Gideon studied the mark on her wrist, then the matching one on his own ribs. “Consent form cut straight to the chase.”

Jian snorted. “Consent is retroactive now? Good to know.” He toggled the recorder clipped to his lapel; a tiny red light blinked twice, then settled. “Seventeen minutes, forty-three seconds. Breach sealed, score uploaded, metabolic signature stabilized.” He glanced at the corridor walls, still breathing shallowly around them. “We’re ghosts in the playlist until the body decides on repeat.”

Corv’s membrane rippled in what might have been amusement. “Ghosts don’t sweat,” he said, nodding toward the bead tracking Gideon’s temple.

“Performance tax,” Gideon answered, wiping it away. “Crowley’s law: every miracle gets invoiced.”

Sona’s fingers brushed her collarbone, pressing once, twice, as if silencing an internal bell. “We still owe the encore,” she said. “I left the last interval unresolved—held back the seventh. The barrier wants closure.”

Jian exhaled through his teeth. “You can’t dangle a leading tone in vascular tissue. The immune system will try to resolve it as threat.”

“That’s why we’re leaving,” she countered. “The body can finish the phrase its own way. We just have to trust it remembers the key.”

“Trust,” Jian repeated, tasting the word. “And if it forgets?”

“Then tomorrow we come back humming,” Gideon said. He flexed his scarred forearm; beneath the skin, the star’s halo of capillary light throbbed once, visible only to him, a private signal he had no intention of sharing. “But for tonight, we walk quiet. No more notes, no more breaches. The audience already paid.”

Corv slid the membrane into a neat coil at his shoulder, violet fading toward the shade of a bruised sky. “Quiet it is,” he agreed. “Quiet enough that the Weavers will think we were never here.”

Sona gave a short, humorless laugh. “Weavers think in hexagons. They’ll notice the gap.”

“Then they’ll write their own countermelody,” Jian said, closing the recorder with a soft click. “We just have to hope it’s in tune.”

The corridor inhaled again, slower this time, as if debating encore against exit. They stood still, letting the hush settle over them like a thin membrane, the aftermath neither apology nor celebration—only the soft biological acknowledgment that the song had lodged somewhere permanent, and the price for retrieval had already begun compounding interest.
