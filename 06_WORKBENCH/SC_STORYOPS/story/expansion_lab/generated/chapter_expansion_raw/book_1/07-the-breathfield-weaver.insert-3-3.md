Sona let the field collapse with the slow grace of a falling curtain, but the resonance refused to die all at once. It clung like the smell of burnt sugar, a sweetness edged with char, and she could feel it pooling behind her teeth whenever she swallowed. The host’s last exhalation had been neither consent nor surrender—only the mechanical reflex of lungs that no longer had a claim on them. That ambiguity worried her more than any outright refusal; it left a debt she could not name.

Corv gave the stilled torso one last clinical glance, then opened the sterile wallet he carried inside his coat. From it he drew a single disposable lancet and a glass ampoule no longer than his thumb. “We need a clean biomark,” he said. “Halden will audit the residue, and if he finds even a whisper of non-native epigenetic drift, he’ll kneecap the program before dawn.” His voice carried a soft vehemence that made the overhead LEDs flicker, as if the building itself flinched.

Sona took the lancet, but Gideon laid a hand over her wrist before she could break the seal. “Mine,” he said. “You’re still mid-cycle. A bleed now would spit cortisol markers all over the signature.” He pressed the point to the ball of his own thumb without ceremony, a soldier donating to his own flag. The bead of blood darkened to a garnet that caught the corridor light like a tiny planet. He let it fall into the ampoule, then held it out between two fingers. “One ticket out of here. Try not to drop it.”

Corv produced a folded square of carbon mesh from the same wallet and laid it on the gurney’s rail like a napkin at some absurd picnic. “Buffer layer,” he explained. “Absorbs the emotional echo so the courier drone doesn’t panic over the Atlantic. The mesh was braided by a failed candidate—Kestrel, I think—three months before she washed out. She called it her ‘good-bye scarf.’ Said it worked better than therapy.”

Sona’s lips twitched. “Kestrel also said the moon was a refugee, remember? Not the most reliable witness.” But she helped Corv ease the mesh under the ampoule cradle, careful not to let the glass clink against metal. The scarf smelled faintly of cedar; someone had stored it in a cedar drawer to keep moths from the weave. Practical superstition, she thought—exactly the sort of small, human absurdity the protocols couldn’t parse.

Gideon sealed the ampoule with a thumb-sized o-ring, then pressed it into a foam socket inside a matte-black transit tube. He clicked the lid twice—once for confirmation, once like a nervous habit—and tucked it into the officer’s open palm. The officer turned the tube over, reading the hazard glyph by the glow of the exit sign. “No tags?” he asked.

“Just your badge number,” Gideon said. “If anyone stops you, you’re courier-class, not witness. Let Halden chase shadows.”

The officer gave a dry huff that might have been a laugh. “Halden doesn’t chase. He waits.” Still, he slid the tube into the inner pocket opposite the toothpick, weighting both sides of his coat like scales.

Corv checked the wall chronometer: a thin violet line pulsing toward an invisible deadline. “Seven minutes before the reassertion handshake. After that, the building re-locks on oversight protocols and we walk out past three layers of biometric gates that won’t recognize our clearance. I’d rather not explain to the duty nurse why my adrenal signature spiked.”

Sona rolled her shoulders, testing the new width of her exhale. The motion felt borrowed, like wearing someone else’s jacket, but the sleeves fit better than she expected. “When we hit the corridor,” she said, “let me take point. If any residual echo tries to ride my pulse, I’ll cough once. That’s the signal to fall back and let the mesh do its job.”

Gideon lifted an eyebrow. “You’re volunteering to be the canary?”

“Someone has to whistle,” she replied. “Besides, the host liked birds.”

The officer cracked the chamber door, and a thin draft of warmer air slipped in—ozone over cedar, resin over blood. He paused, head cocked like a man listening for a song just beyond range. “Your mother,” he said to the empty room behind them, “would’ve hated this part.” The words carried no answer, only the soft click of the latch as it throttled the light down to a seam.
