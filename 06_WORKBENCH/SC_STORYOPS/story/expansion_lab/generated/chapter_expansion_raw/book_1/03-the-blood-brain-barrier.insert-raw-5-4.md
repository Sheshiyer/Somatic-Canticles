Gideon’s wrist-comp vibrates once—quiet, almost polite. He flips the lid with his thumb: a single amber glyph pulsing like a slow heartbeat.  
“Ketoacid drift,” he murmurs. “Fleet wants us to vent the coil if the pH drops another point-two.”  

Corv snorts. “Fleet can suck my point-two. Coil’s already running hotter than a warlock’s honeymoon.” He kneels, fingers sliding along the silver helix until the metal whispers back, a thin, obedient sigh. “I can coax another four minutes before the weave frays. After that, we’re breathing carbonized hope.”  

Jian leans against the wall, helmet lamp tilted so the light carves her cheekbone into something sharp enough to sign treaties on. “Four minutes is a haiku, not a plan.”  

Gideon opens his mouth, closes it again. The corridor’s elastic floor flexes under the shift of his boot; he feels the pulse travel up his shin like rumor. Somewhere behind them, a minute seismic tremor—just enough to remind him the barrier is alive, bored, and waiting for excuses.  

Corv flicks his thumbnail against the coil. A single green spark leaps, dies. “Tell you what, Captain Tight-Lips. I’ll trade you the four minutes for one honest answer.”  

“Define honest.”  

“Start with why your pupils just dilated when the glyph hit. We all saw it.”  

Gideon exhales through his teeth. “Fleet’s running silent simulations on the lattice. They’re… testing collapse thresholds.”  

Jian’s eyebrow twitches. “Translating: if we break it, they learn how long the rest of the fleet survives. Cute.”  

“Not cute,” Gideon says. “Necessary.”  

Corv laughs, low, almost fond. “Spoken like a man who’s already written our eulogies.”  

The corridor dims, a slow bleed to violet. The coil answers with a harmonic that feels like a bruise. Corv’s shoulders stiffen. “We’re down to two minutes. Decision window’s closing like a guillotine made of fog.”  

Gideon feels the air thin—not breathless, just decision-dense. He toggles the wrist-comp, kills the amber glyph. “We don’t vent. We ride the weave until it sings or snaps.”  

“Risky,” Jian says.  

“Risk is the only currency the barrier accepts.” He glances at her sideways, half-smile, half-grimace. “Besides, you still owe me a favor from Europa. Consider this collection.”  

Jian rolls her eyes, but the tension in her jaw eases a millimeter. She taps her helmet mic twice—an old superstition from academy days, a quiet knock against fate.  

Corv draws a slow circle on the floor with the tip of the coil. The violet light gathers there, pooling like spilled ink. “Thirty seconds to recalibrate. I need absolute silence or the phase slips. Anyone breathes loud, I start reciting Crowley in limerick form.”  

Gideon raises both hands, palms out. Jian follows suit, mock solemn. The corridor holds its breath with them.  

Nothing moves except the coil’s faint shimmer—silver threads braiding themselves tighter, counting heartbeats in base-phi. Gideon counts too, silently: twenty-eight, twenty-nine… At thirty, Corv lifts two fingers, victory and warning in one lazy salute.  

“Harmonic locked,” he whispers. “We just bought thirty seconds. Thirty-three if she blinks.”
