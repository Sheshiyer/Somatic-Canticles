# Chapter 6: The Synaptic Crossroads

The ambient lighting inside the Manas Interface dropped two Kelvin when the redirected signature surfaced, as if the Anamnesis Engine itself was bracing for an audit. Jian tasted copper on the side of his tongue—the alert that the system had escalated to Level-3 resonance. He glanced at the wrist sliver on Sona’s glove: same taste, same timing. They had crossed a metabolic threshold; their bodies already knew before the HUD confirmed it.

Corv exhaled through his teeth, a quiet hiss that passed for laughter among the Somanauts. “The pituitary’s ghost just unpacked its own suitcase,” he said. “Now it wants to know which exit we’ll use.”

None of them laughed. The split had already begun.

Black silence widened around the four bodies, then the Engine rendered the verdict. A fork of impossible clarity bloomed in midair, two luminous highways braided from the same severed trunk. One rose in cool, translucent blue, its dendritic branches tessellated like frost on glass. The other writhed arterial red, wet and breathing.

The Synaptic Crossroads.

Corv’s first step was purely optical; he rotated his head so the lenses could triangulate the angle of divergence. “Endocrine law, same old trick,” he murmured. “Generate load, monetize the story around it, call that equilibrium.”

Jian’s HUD lit with the rosettes of a neocortical atlas: Brodmann areas glowing like city grids from orbit. He let the map settle over his vision before he spoke. “Then we follow the story-layer,” he said. “Pain is noise; narrative is signal.”

Sona had already turned away from him. The red branch billowed against her face, raising sweat along the line of her jaw. She could taste the grief in the corridor—iron, woodsmoke, the particular pH of childhood hospitals. She hated how easily memory scavenged the body. “Smoke is not signal,” she said. “Smoke means something is burning.”

Jian’s reply came clipped, almost polite. “And combustion without containment spreads until it starves itself. Structure is not betrayal.”

Gideon widened his stance between them, boots magnetized to the floor grid. His Klei Toda’ah unfurled, micro-filaments of living mesh knitting a sphere just wide enough to keep their heartbeats from syncing. The gesture looked gentle, but the acoustics shifted; Sona’s pulse dropped eight beats per minute as soon as the field stabilized. Gideon did not glance at either of them. “Clock’s running,” he said. “Two minutes until the fork hardens and picks for us.”

Corv dragged a gloved finger across the air, sketching a tangent that avoided both branches. “Listen to the architecture,” he said. “The choice is a decoy. The system is defending the wound by giving us clean binary options. Choose either route and the source remains untouched.”

Jian adjusted the focal length on his retinal HUD. The blue lattice sharpened into vertebrae of cascading cortical explanations: “reframe memory loop,” “attach locus of control,” “update self-schema.” Each node came with a citation tag from the Quoril Field Manual. He almost smiled. “Our tools were built to dismantle this,” he said. “We can walk the logic tree, excise the faulty premise, collapse the whole justification cascade.”

Sona angled her body so the red branch’s wind struck her side-on. She needed the heat without the blindness. “And tomorrow the same premise regrows,” she said. “Because the body remembers what the cortex refuses to archive.”

Jian’s gaze flicked down to her boots—anchored, balanced, already breathing in the storm rhythm. He felt a prickle of envy at how quickly she located the pulse inside chaos. “Raw affect offers no vector,” he said.

“Wrong scale,” she shot back. “You’re inspecting the waveform when you should be tracing the current that keeps generating it.”

They were no longer arguing about routes; they were testing the same old fracture point. Jian heard the edge in her voice and recognized it as the exact frequency they had first discovered six months ago in training sim, the day they almost failed team sync. The memory blinked across his limbic HUD as a 9.2-millisecond spike marked *shame*. He killed the alert. Not useful.

Gideon’s Klei flexed once, a deliberate micro-contraction that reset their pheromonic overlay. “Vent the quarrel,” he said. “Or it will become the path.”

Sona inhaled through clenched teeth, then let it out in a slow, deliberate sigh. “Fine,” she said. “Let’s measure what refuses measurement.” She closed her eyes, shutting down visual input entirely. The red corridor rushed in through other doors: the low roar of blood in the ears, the granular taste of adrenaline, the sub-audible tremor she had learned to read as the body’s best attempt at honesty. Hunting not the loudest wave, but the one that returned.

There. A cadence, almost hidden beneath arrhythmic surges of grief. Three-beat, rest, two-beat. Then again. She synced her exhalations to it, not to reduce it but to prove it was regular.

“Jian,” she said, eyes still closed. “Tell me what you see at forty Hertz.”

He re-tuned the scanner before suspicion could form words. The limbic corridor was a morass of high-amplitude spikes, sure, but beneath them a low-frequency carrier pulsed at 0.8 Hz—precisely the delta band reported in prenatal memory consolidation. His throat tightened. “Stable periodicity,” he admitted. “Not artifact.” He hated how much relief the number gave him. “It’s directional.”

Sona opened her eyes and found his gaze already waiting, no longer looking past her. “So the fire still remembers the match,” she said.

Jian snorted—an involuntary half-laugh that cracked the tension like thin ice. “I was going to say ‘data indicates non-random oscillation,’ but yours is cleaner.”

Corv watched the exchange without turning. “That is the move,” he said. “Not triumph of feeling over logic. Contact before commentary. Signal before story.”

Sona stepped forward until the red heat pressed flat against her sternum. She felt the rhythm against bone now, not metaphorically—an actual 0.8 hertz thud, like standing inside a heart the size of a continent. It steadied her more than Gideon’s Klei had. She spoke without looking back. “We go where the carrier lives.”

Jian did not hesitate this time. He mapped the pulse onto the shared HUD, overlaying the delta wave as a luminous rail that the limbic storm could not wash away. “Path is trackable,” he said. “If coherence drops by three percent, I call abort.”

Gideon withdrew the Klei’s outer layers, shrinking the buffer zone so they could feel the full thermal gradient without being incinerated. “I’ll hold the perimeter,” he said. “Don’t let the noise pick your stride.”

Corv pivoted so his shoulder brushed the edge of the blue branch—just enough to register how fiercely it begged them back. Elegant counter-arguments flared in pale cyan: *Risk minimized*, *Cognitive closure*, *Long-term stability*. They looked almost beautiful. He dipped a finger into one flare, and it wrapped around him like silk promising safe passage home. He withdrew his hand before it could seal.

“The doctrine breeds explanations the way wounds breed pus,” he said. “It keeps the injury from mortifying, but it also keeps the shrapnel inside.” He aligned with Sona and Jian, the three of them now forming a narrow wedge pointed into the red. “We follow the interruption before the system re-buries it.”

The red branch widened as they approached. Heat became sound: a low, guttural chant made entirely of vowels too old for consonants. Gideon walked point, Klei now compressed into a thin lattice that looked like scale mail made of light. Sona walked second, palms open, letting the pulse guide her feet. Jian walked third, eyes fixed on the rail of delta he had projected ahead, watching for the first tremor of decoherence. Corv walked last, collecting the echoes the others left behind—raw affections, diagnostic footnotes, stray jokes—compressing them into the datalog like evidence that still hurt.

Ten meters in, the corridor narrowed to a tunnel. Temperature spiked again, but now it carried scent: iodine, dust from the first apartment she had ever cried in, the metallic edge of hospital handrails. Sona’s knees buckled for a half-step. Gideon’s hand brushed the small of her back—no more weight than a thought—steady enough for her to find her stance again.

Jian’s HUD lit amber. “Coherence drop,” he said. “Two point seven.”

“Surface tension,” Corv said. “System’s first counterpunch.”

They adjusted: Gideon widened the Klei just enough to cool the microclimate around Sona’s face, Jian recalculated the carrier phase to compensate for the Doppler shift caused by their own forward motion. No one slowed.

Another five meters and the tunnel forked again. This time the branches were biological: a carotid and a jugular, each wide enough to walk inside. The pulse they had been tracing now came from the left channel, slow and heavy as planetary tides.

Sona reached the fork first, placed one hand on the warm arterial wall. It pulsed against her palm like a horse’s neck after a hard run. She felt the old vertigo of standing too close to something alive that had never agreed to language. “Carotid,” she said. “Upstream to the source.”

Jian’s scanner flared red. “Velocity spike,” he warned. “If the pressure gradient climbs above—”

“Then we ride it,” Gideon cut in. He angled his shoulder so the surge would carry them rather than flatten them. “Speed is now a resource.”

Corv laughed once, a short bark that startled even himself. “System’s final gambit: make us fear the very current that can take us home.”

They stepped into the artery. The walls contracted around them, not as threat but as invitation. Warm blood rushing past their faces smelled of iron, seawater, and the impossible sweetness of fresh bread. The pulse they had tracked now became their own. Synchronization was no longer metaphor; their heartbeats phased-locked to the buried rhythm at exactly 0.8 Hz.

Sona tasted salt at the back of her throat and realized she was crying. No shame in it. Tears adjusted ocular pH so the lenses could read higher wavelengths. Biology repurposing emotion into instrumentation. She let the tears stay.

Jian watched his HUD: the carrier wave now riding them rather than the other way around. He spoke quietly, more to himself than the others. “Data integrity holding. We are inside the signal, not decoding it from the outside.” His voice carried something almost tender.

Gideon tracked the curvature ahead: the artery bending upward, narrowing, the walls growing translucent until they could see light beyond them. “Prepare for breach,” he said. “Exit velocity unknown.”

Corv reached forward, tapped Sona’s shoulder twice in silent recognition. She covered his hand with hers for one beat. Then all four of them leaned into the final systolic surge.

They shot through the arterial wall and landed in a space too quiet to measure. The temperature was exactly skin-neutral. The light was the color of closed eyelids in noon sun. At the center hovered a single node: the suppressed signature from the pituitary chamber, no longer encrypted, no longer split. A silver droplet turning slowly, reflecting each of their faces at once.

Sona’s breath caught. “It looks like a tear that learned how to stay suspended.”

Jian’s scans stripped the droplet down to protocol: amplitude, frequency, phase, coherence length. Then he flipped the HUD away and looked with naked eyes. The silver surface held a micro-constellation of scars—old ones, surgical, deliberate. “We were never meant to find this,” he said. “We were meant to forget it.”

Gideon said nothing. He simply brought the Klei to full expansion, forming a sphere of neutral pressure around the node so nothing could re-encrypt it while they decided what came next.

Corv’s voice dropped to the register he used when he wanted to be overheard by the machine itself. “Here is the interruption the doctrine buried,” he said. “Choose now how we reintroduce it to the body politic.”

Sona stepped to the edge of Gideon’s sphere, close enough to see her own face floating in the silver droplet. She thought of the boy she had been, the night she first realized pain could be bureaucratically denied. She spoke directly to the droplet, as if to the part of herself it represented. “I will not translate you into policy,” she said. “But I will not leave you in myth, either.”

Jian reached out, let the tip of one finger hover just shy of contact. “Then we annotate, not overwrite,” he said. “One footnote at a time.”

Gideon lowered the Klei’s field enough to let Sona’s tears drift forward and touch the surface. Where the saltwater met silver, the droplet shivered and grew a second skin—a thin membrane now capable of resonance with the present tense. The carrier wave they had ridden now modulated into audible range: a low, steady heartbeat, no longer buried.

Corv closed the datalog with a soft click. “Entry logged,” he said. “Let the record show the interruption survived first contact.”

They stood in a hush so absolute they could hear the micro-crackle of their own synapses adapting to what they had carried home. Sona felt the rhythm settle into her chest like a guest who had always lived there, only now allowed to speak its name.

Outside the neutral sphere, the red corridor and the blue corridor had vanished. In their place lay a single hallway, its walls lined with mirrored stillness. No more forced choice. Just the long walk back toward the surface, carrying the signal they had agreed to neither worship nor bury.

Jian adjusted the HUD to map the correct route upward. Every route now pointed the same direction: exit through the same flesh that had let them in. He glanced at Sona. “Ready to become the annotation?” he asked.

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, streaking salt across her cheek like warpaint. “Only if you promise to footnote every time you try to outrun your own heartbeat.”

He surprised himself by laughing—short, almost shy. “Deal,” he said, and for a moment the laugh echoed in the mirrored walls, multiplying until it sounded like the whole nervous system laughing with them.

Corv started walking. The mirrored hallway accepted their reflections and then let them go, one footfall at a time. Gideon kept the Klei at a low idle, not healing, just witnessing. Sona followed, palm over the slow steady drum in her chest. Jian walked last, recording everything, no longer trying to get ahead of the rhythm.

The silver droplet’s second skin pulsed once, not with a beat but with a question: *now that you have carried me out of myth, how will you keep from converting me into another legend?* The hush deepened until even the micro-crackle of their synapses felt loud. Sona’s hand, still floating beside the sphere, curled into a loose fist as though the empty space itself had weight.

Gideon was the first to register the consequence. The Klei’s field sensors, tuned to detect any attempt at re-encryption, flickered a soft violet—never a color he had trained for. Not attack; not defense. Uncertainty. He exhaled through his teeth, the same quiet hiss Corv used for laughter, but slower. “The Engine is listening,” he said. “Not with microphones. With memory pressure. Every step we take upward will test whether the heartbeat scales or compresses.” His HUD showed a simple curve: baseline coherence versus time, climbing toward a crest that hadn’t been modeled. “We have a bandwidth cap,” he added, almost apologetic. “If the signal outruns our biology, it folds back into noise.”

Jian’s fingers twitched above the HUD controls. He had already begun drafting an annotation layer—tiny floating glyphs that would anchor the droplet’s new resonance in the body’s own dialect, somewhere between neurotransmitter and footnote. Yet the glyphs kept collapsing into simpler symbols, as if the system itself preferred hieroglyph to footnote. He swore under his breath. “Syntax erosion,” he said. “The closer we get to the cortical gate, the more the language thins.”

Corv angled his head, listening past the heartbeat to the silence behind it. “Thin language is honest language,” he said. “Let it become pictograph. Let it become silence. The doctrine fears silence more than contradiction.” He reached into his coat, withdrew a thin obsidian shard no bigger than a guitar pick—failed candidacy memorabilia, edge still sharp enough to score glass. He balanced it on his thumbnail. “When we reach the surface, they’ll ask for a story. We could hand them this instead.” The shard caught the neutral light and fractured it into two colors neither red nor blue. “A mute witness,” he added. “Harder to weaponize.”

Sona watched the reflection of her tear glide across the droplet’s surface, now a miniature ocean with its own lunar pull. She felt its gravity in her sternum, a downward tug that made her lungs expand as though to receive deeper weather. She spoke to the shard, not to Corv. “Any edge sharp enough to cut story is sharp enough to cut bearer,” she said. Her voice was soft enough that only the heartbeat answered, accelerating one half-step, then settling. “I’d rather carry the dull edge. The one that bruises but doesn’t sever.”

Gideon adjusted the Klei one last time, shrinking the sphere until it fit like a second skin around the droplet, no larger than a closed fist. The field now emitted a faint warmth—no longer neutral, but intimate, a lullaby without melody. “Minimal footprint,” he said. “If we’re going to smuggle memory past the gate, we pack it small enough to fit inside a breath.” He glanced at Sona. “You’ll hold it.” It wasn’t a question or an order; it was a calibration. Sona’s resting tremor measured 1.8 micro-g—ideal for resonance coupling. She nodded once, the motion tiny, precise.

The mirrored hallway waited ahead, perfectly silent, its floor already reflecting their next footfall before they took it. Jian killed the HUD annotations, leaving only the raw delta trace scrolling like a single line of text they would have to read aloud with their bodies. He slipped the wrist sliver into his pocket; the copper taste faded from his tongue, replaced by something quieter: the flavor of paper left in sunlight. “Ready for the ascent,” he said. His voice came out steadier than he felt.

Corv turned the obsidian shard once more between finger and thumb, then tucked it into Sona’s free hand. “In case the story insists,” he whispered. “You can always break the mirror instead of answering.”

Sona closed her fist around the shard—not tight, just enough to feel its coolness equalize with her own temperature. The heartbeat inside the sphere nudged against her ribs, like a cat asking to be carried. She inhaled, and the rhythm slipped into her diaphragm, setting the cadence for the first step. The mirrored hallway accepted the footfall without echo, as though the floor were water absorbing its own ripples.

Gideon pinched the Klei down to a sphere no bigger than a cherry pit, then glanced at the corridor’s chronometer—forty-three seconds ahead of the drift-window.  
“Clock’s bragging,” he muttered. “Let’s make it regret the math.”

Jian tapped the side of his skull where the HUD had been. “Every millisecond we shave is one less excuse the gate has to notice us.”  
He looked to Sona. “Consent check? You’re the only one who hasn’t spoken today.”

Sona flexed her left hand, the tremor now almost imperceptible. “Spoke plenty in the elevator. Just used subvocal; you weren’t listening.”  
She gave her wrist a micro-roll, showing the delta trace fluttering like a moth under the skin. “I’m in. But this heartbeat isn’t a puppy. If it starts whining in transit, I drop it—no argument.”

Corv lifted the obsidian shard between thumb and forefinger, its surface catching the corridor’s ghost light. “If it whines, break me instead. I’m cheaper to patch.”  
He flicked the shard once; the single note rang like a nail dragged across glass. “Sound check. Gate hates surprises with teeth.”

Gideon drew the sphere to the edge of his sleeve, letting the faint warmth meet the cooler delta field. The two temperatures negotiated a thin, audible hiss—air sucked through a crack too small to see.  
“That’s the handshake,” he said. “Body heat meets memory heat. After that, we’re just three sacrificial commas in the sentence it wants to finish.”

Jian exhaled through his teeth, tasting again the ghost of sun-bleached paper. “You realize repeating the metaphor makes the gate think we’re stalling.”  
He unclipped the cuff of his glove, revealing a pale band of skin still indented by the neural lace. “I’ll carry the margin. Sona carries the story. Corv carries the blade. Gideon writes the footnote.”

Sona angled her head, considering. “Division of labor is cute, but the heartbeat doesn’t read memos. It’ll latch onto whoever’s pulse skips first.”  
She lifted her free hand in a half-salute. “So let’s synchronize like polite assassins.”

Corv touched two fingers to the pulse at Sona’s throat, counting silently. “One-Mississippi. Two-Mississippi. Your left ventricle is showing off.”  
He withdrew before she could swat him. “Relax. I’m calibrating, not flirting.”

Gideon slid the Klei sphere into the center of his palm, then tipped it toward Sona. “Catch.”  
The sphere rolled across his lifeline like a dark marble, stopping just short of her cupped hand. She accepted it without friction, the tremor flattening to zero as the warmth transferred.  
“Held,” she said. “Temperature steady. No leakage. Pride intact.”

Jian watched the corridor swallow their reflections footfall by footfall. “We move on the count of three. Count starts now.”  
He paused, letting the silence stretch until it felt almost like laughter. “One.”  
Corv inhaled. “Two.”  
Sona closed her fist around the sphere. “Two-point-nine.”  
Gideon smiled, small, sharp. “Close enough.”

“Sync drift at minus three microvolts,” Jian murmurs, eyes on the cuff-mounted monitor. “Damp the baroreflex, Sona.”  
Sona exhales through her teeth, shoulders dropping a measured centimeter. “If I damp any harder I’ll faint. Check your own vagal tone.”

Corv’s glove glints as he tweaks a micro-dial on the inside of his wrist. “Heart-rate variance dropping across the board. We’re flattening out. Gideon, you’re lagging.”  
Gideon taps the Klei sphere once, a soft click against the metacarpal plate. “Artifact’s feeding back. I’ll ride the lag—keeps the beat honest.”

The corridor lights flicker, not failure but scheduled dimming to reduce retinal after-image retention. Sona’s pupils bloom. “Thirty seconds to neural aperture. We need a hard parity check.”  
Jian nods, already sliding the flat of his thumb across the team’s shared vitals ribbon. “Cross-feed: my delta to Corv, his theta to Sona, Sona’s gamma to Gideon. Loop closes on my mark.”  
He waits. The silence is a low electric hum, almost warm.

Corv breaks it, soft. “Remember the mock run in Kyoto? You puked at 0.7 sync.”  
Sona snorts, tension bleeding. “I puked because you microwaved fish in the ready room. That’s on you, crow-boy.”  
Gideon’s mouth quirks. “File under contamination protocol.”

Jian lifts one finger—no drama, just data. “Mark.”  
The four streams braid in the cuff display: green, green, amber, green. A single amber blip winks twice, steadies.  
“Parity locked,” Jian says. “We walk inside one another’s refractory periods now. Do not break ranks for anything short of cardiac arrest.”  
Corv grins, quick. “If anyone arrests, we rotate who gets the dramatic last words. I call dibs.”  
Sona rolls her eyes, but the motion is loose and the tremor stays gone.

The mirrored hallway ended sooner than any of them expected, folding into a small chamber whose walls were the matte black of a pupil in bright light. No echo, no reflection—just an absence that drank every footfall. At its center, a single pressure pad glowed the color of capillary blood. Above it, a narrow vent exhaled a scent equal parts antiseptic and campfire ash.

Sona halted first, the Klei sphere cupped against her sternum like a second heart rehearsing its debut. The warmth had migrated—no longer just the sphere’s heat, but a returned pulse that felt older than the body holding it. She measured the interval with the back of her tongue: still 0.8 Hz, but the gain had doubled. Each cycle rode the hinge of her jaw as though reminding her who truly carried whom.

“Micro-tear at the sphere’s equator,” Gideon said, voice low enough to qualify as confession. “Stress fracture from the systolic push. Integrity at ninety-one percent and dropping.” He did not look at Sona; he looked at the fracture’s reflection in her pupils instead.

Corv leaned closer, thumb tracing a ghost circle around the fracture without touching. “Material memory,” he murmured. “The sphere remembers how much force it took to get us here. It’s negotiating amnesty.” He glanced at Jian. “Do we grant it?”

Jian already had the cuff ribbon open, delta trace scrolling. The carrier wave had begun to wobble—three degrees phase drift, then four. “We grant nothing yet,” he said. “We stabilize. I can rephase the carrier by injecting a controlled arrhythmia across the group. Two beats off, three beats on. Everyone will feel it.”

Sona’s throat flexed. “Last time you tried controlled arrhythmia in sim, Corv coded for twelve seconds.”

“Sim didn’t have an exit wound,” Jian replied. He lifted his left hand, showing the underside of the wrist where a small vein stood blue and urgent. “I’ll take the first skipped beat. Anyone else volunteers?”

Corv flexed his fingers, the obsidian shard pinned between two knuckles. “Give me the skip. I like how silence tastes just before the body remembers it isn’t dead.”

Gideon caught Sona’s gaze, asking without words. She shook her head once—minute, final. “No skips. We ride the tear.” She turned the sphere so the fracture faced outward, a thin white line catching the vent’s faint red glow. “If the sphere breaks, the heartbeat scatters. Good. Memory should not travel in one intact piece. Too easy to archive, too easy to forget.”

Jian’s HUD flickered amber. Phase drift five degrees. “Decision window closes in fifteen seconds.”

Sona closed her free hand over the fracture, feeling the heat sting. “Decision is made. We breach the pad together, no stutter step. When the sphere ruptures, each of us catches what sticks. No pooling, no curation. Raw scatter.”

Corv’s grin flickered, half mischief, half relief. “Democracy by shrapnel. I can work with that.”

Gideon adjusted the Klei one last time, thinning it until the sphere appeared to float above Sona’s palm, fracture gleaming like a fault line in moonlight. “On three,” he said, “we drop our weight onto the pad. Sphere cracks against sternum, heartbeat disperses, we keep moving before the Engine drafts a rescue protocol.”

Jian tapped the ribbon twice, locking the delta trace so the wave could collapse without telemetry. “One.”

Sona inhaled, tasting salt and iron and the ghost of hospital blankets. “Two.”

Corv pressed the shard against the sphere’s fracture—not cutting, just marking the exact vector. “Two-point-nine.”

Gideon exhaled. “Close enough.”

They stepped.

The pad gave beneath their combined mass with the soft resistance of living cartilage. The sphere cracked along the scored line, releasing a pulse of silver light that split into four ribbons. Each ribbon struck a sternum, sank under the rib line, and vanished.

For one suspended heartbeat the chamber had no walls, no vent, no floor—only the afterimage of a communal systole that felt like the entire team inhaling at once.

Then the chamber snapped back. Lights steadied. The vent exhaled one last breath, this time odorless.

Sona’s tremor was gone. Gideon’s lag had flattened. Corv’s resting pulse read two beats below baseline—the lowest since induction. Jian’s ribbon showed four green lines, no delta trace left to chase.

They looked at one another without speaking, the shared silence louder than laughter.

Corv slipped the obsidian shard into the empty hollow where the sphere had been, a small black seed in place of a heart. “For later,” he said. “In case we forget whose fracture this was.”

Jian closed the cuff, sealing the vitals ribbon into the permanent log. The amber light winked out.

Together they turned toward the single exit that had appeared opposite the pad: a plain metal door without handle, sensor plate, or label. It stood ajar by two centimeters, enough to let through a draft that smelled of nothing at all.

Sona led this time, heartbeat in her chest now entirely her own.

Corv pinched the bridge of his nose, still tasting copper from the last pulse-spike. “We’re about to walk out of our own nervous system and pretend we remember how to breathe normal air. Anyone want to vote on which of us leaks dreams first?”

Jian’s reply was half cough, half laugh. “Statistics favor the empath. Sona already has the exit stamp on her cortex.”

Sona lifted the vitals ribbon like a wilted flower. “The stamp says ‘property of the Engine.’ I’d like my heartbeat back before customs confiscates it.”

Corv glanced at the ribbon’s amber readout, still scrolling micro-oscillations. “One problem: the Engine wrote its signature on every QRS complex. If we detach now, we’ll flat-line for eight seconds—just long enough to forget why we came.”

Jian tapped the cuff release, then paused. “Eight seconds is the legal definition of death in three jurisdictions. I’d rather not test the revival clauses tonight.”

Corv rolled his shoulders, the motion sending a static snap across the mesh of his jacket. “Fine. We keep the leash until we hit daylight. But the moment we see sky, I’m cutting the feed and taking my arrhythmia private.”

Sona tucked the ribbon inside her collar, where the pulse warmed her clavicle like a guilty secret. “Private won’t last. The Engine’s already indexing this conversation, tagging affect, archiving hesitation.”

Jian’s fingers drummed against the sealed cuff. “Then we give it nothing to chew on. Monosyllables from here to the threshold. Think of it as emotional fasting.”

Corv snorted. “You try fasting when the buffet is your own limbic system.”

A soft chime sounded from the pad—three descending notes that tasted of regret. The platform lights dimmed to a bruised violet, signaling the last dregs of local power. Somewhere below, the Engine’s dream-caches were flushing their buffers, ready to rewrite the corridor they stood in as soon as they stepped off it.

Jian exhaled through his teeth. “Exit window’s shrinking. We either leave now or wake up tomorrow wearing someone else’s nostalgia.”

Corv lifted the obsidian shard, turning it so the violet light fractured across its matte surface. “The sphere left us this souvenir. Any theories?”

Sona studied the shard without reaching for it. “It’s the Engine’s idea of a receipt. Proof we paid with a memory we can’t remember losing.”

Jian’s voice dropped to the calm register he used for preflight checks. “We don’t have to take it. We can log it as hazardous bioware and walk away clean.”

Corv’s knuckles whitened around the fragment. “Clean? We left clean behind the first time we let a dream scalp us. I’m keeping the shard so I know which scar to blame.”

Sona stepped between them, palm up. “Consent protocol. We vote. Majority carries, no abstentions.”

Jian nodded. “Motion to jettison the shard.”

Corv countered. “Motion to retain for forensic analysis.”

Both turned to Sona. She felt the phantom thud of the Engine’s borrowed heartbeat syncing with her own, an off-tempo echo that made her ribs itch.

She spoke softly. “Retention. But conditional. The shard stays sealed until we reach a neutral lab. If it leaks so much as one image, we incinerate the rest of the archive and take the amnesia tax.”

Corv flicked an eyebrow. “Severe, but fair. Jian?”

Jian’s smile was thin, almost respectful. “Conditional retention carries. Motion logged.”

The pad beneath them shivered, a subsonic growl that traveled up bone rather than ear. The exit door—still only a dark vertical slit—widened another millimeter, as if the corridor itself was impatient.

Corv loosened his grip on the shard and slipped it into the hollow where the sphere had once nested. The fit was exact; the shard clicked home with the satisfied sigh of memory finding its socket. The violet light guttered, then steadied, calmed by the insertion.

Jian’s gaze flicked to the vitals ribbon. “Heart rate dropping to sync baseline. Looks like the Engine approves our compromise.”

Sona touched the ribbon at her throat, feeling the Engine’s pulse slow to match hers—no, faster now, accelerating in quiet triumph. “It thinks it won,” she whispered.

Corv grinned without warmth. “Let it think. The first rule of parasitism: never let the host notice the siphon until you’re already gone.”

He knelt, running a finger along the seam where the platform met the corridor. A faint dusting of neural ash came away on his glove—evidence of the Engine’s recent editing sweep. He brushed it off, watching the particles dissolve into the violet haze.
