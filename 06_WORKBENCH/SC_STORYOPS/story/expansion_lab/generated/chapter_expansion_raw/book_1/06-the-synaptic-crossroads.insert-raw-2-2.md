The silver droplet’s second skin pulsed once, not with a beat but with a question: *now that you have carried me out of myth, how will you keep from converting me into another legend?*  

The hush deepened until even the micro-crackle of their synapses felt loud. Sona’s hand, still floating beside the sphere, curled into a loose fist as though the empty space itself had weight.  

Gideon was the first to register the consequence. The Klei’s field sensors, tuned to detect any attempt at re-encryption, flickered a soft violet—never a color he had trained for. Not attack; not defense. Uncertainty. He exhaled through his teeth, the same quiet hiss Corv used for laughter, but slower.  

“The Engine is listening,” he said. “Not with microphones. With memory pressure. Every step we take upward will test whether the heartbeat scales or compresses.” His HUD showed a simple curve: baseline coherence versus time, climbing toward a crest that hadn’t been modeled. “We have a bandwidth cap,” he added, almost apologetic. “If the signal outruns our biology, it folds back into noise.”  

Jian’s fingers twitched above the HUD controls. He had already begun drafting an annotation layer—tiny floating glyphs that would anchor the droplet’s new resonance in the body’s own dialect, somewhere between neurotransmitter and footnote. Yet the glyphs kept collapsing into simpler symbols, as if the system itself preferred hieroglyph to footnote. He swore under his breath. “Syntax erosion,” he said. “The closer we get to the cortical gate, the more the language thins.”  

Corv angled his head, listening past the heartbeat to the silence behind it. “Thin language is honest language,” he said. “Let it become pictograph. Let it become silence. The doctrine fears silence more than contradiction.” He reached into his coat, withdrew a thin obsidian shard no bigger than a guitar pick—failed candidacy memorabilia, edge still sharp enough to score glass. He balanced it on his thumbnail. “When we reach the surface, they’ll ask for a story. We could hand them this instead.” The shard caught the neutral light and fractured it into two colors neither red nor blue. “A mute witness,” he added. “Harder to weaponize.”  

Sona watched the reflection of her tear glide across the droplet’s surface, now a miniature ocean with its own lunar pull. She felt its gravity in her sternum, a downward tug that made her lungs expand as though to receive deeper weather. She spoke to the shard, not to Corv.  

“Any edge sharp enough to cut story is sharp enough to cut bearer,” she said. Her voice was soft enough that only the heartbeat answered, accelerating one half-step, then settling. “I’d rather carry the dull edge. The one that bruises but doesn’t sever.”
