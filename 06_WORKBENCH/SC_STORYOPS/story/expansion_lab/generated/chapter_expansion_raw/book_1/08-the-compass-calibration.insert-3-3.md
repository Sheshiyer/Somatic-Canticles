Jian waited for the silence to settle, then let the silence wait for him.

“It’s too quiet,” he said, not to Sona, but to the corridor. The walls answered with a low, ventricular hush, as if the moon outside had sucked all the throb from the plumbing. He flexed his left hand; the glove still felt like someone else’s skin had been stitched to his tendons. “Tell me again why we’re trusting a device that flinches at body heat.”

“Because it doesn’t flinch,” Sona replied, touching the dial face with the pad of one thumb, the way a doctor tests for fever. “It listens. Heat is a confession.”

“Yeah, well I confess I’m warm,” Jian muttered. “And sweaty. And possibly leaking cortisol in a pattern the calibration will regret tomorrow.”

Sona’s eyes came up slow, as though they traveled a long private stair. “Regret is the wrong tense. The Compass doesn’t forecast, it narrates. Regret is what we’ll edit into the log afterward.”

He snorted, then regretted the snort—too loud, too much echo. “Fine. Narrate us somewhere that doesn’t smell like cyclohexane and dread.”

She angled the dial a few degrees. The needle bobbed once, like a conductor’s baton acknowledging a wrong entrance, then stilled. “It wants a witness,” she said. “The protocol says we stay present until the last variable converges.”

Jian scratched beneath the edge of his visor where the sweat pooled. “Protocol once told me to floss with optic fiber. I still have nightmares about splinters.”

“Different department,” Sona said, but her mouth flickered. Humor as pressure-release, effortless as always. He envied the way she could make a joke land without ever seeming to aim.

They stood in the dim spill of the corridor’s last functioning lamp. Somewhere behind them, the failed candidate’s boot prints had already begun to fade—rubber residue lifting in microscopic curls, the station’s lungs claiming everything back. He pictured the words the man had scrawled on the wall in grease pencil: *The glacier always wins.* The janitors would paint over it by morning, but the sentence would keep reappearing in the crawl of Jian’s dreams, a looping subtitle he couldn’t mute.

Jian cleared his throat. “You ever wonder if the Compass is just lonely? All that precision, nobody to share it with.”

Sona’s gaze softened; she did not look at the device but past it, into the conditional future. “Instruments aren’t lonely. Operators are.”

He hated how true that sounded. “So we’re the ones who need therapy. Great. Remind me to invoice the moon.”

She adjusted her stance, weight shifting from heel to ball, the small choreography of someone preparing to speak something risky. “Jian, before we lock the bearing… the candidate—did you smell the ketones on him?”

He felt his stomach fold. “Yeah. Acetone over sweat. Liver dumping emergency sugar. He’d gone catabolic.”

“That’s not what I mean.” Sona’s voice lowered, as if the corridor itself might report them. “I mean did you smell the *willingness.* He wanted the ice to take him.”

Jian swallowed. The memory of the man’s eyes—flat, already opaque like clouded quartz—pressed against the inside of his skull. “Protocol says we log, we don’t rescue.”

“Protocol also says we calibrate with informed consent.” She tapped the dial. “Consent is recursive. If the next mind on the wire doesn’t want to come back, the whole route bifurcates.”

He felt the subtle tug of consequence, like a dental hook behind the eye. “So we vote again?”

“We already voted. One abstained by walking north. That leaves two.”

He exhaled through pursed lips, making a reed of his own tension. “Fine. I vote we finish what the candidate started, minus the self-dissolve. But if the Compass pings suicidal ideation again, we pull the plug, no matter whose thesis is on the line.”

Sona studied him, head tilted, a half-smile he hadn’t earned. “Spoken like someone who’s read the footnotes on grief.”

“Grief footnotes are cheaper than therapy co-pays,” he shot back. Then, quieter, “I just don’t want to haul another ghost back to base.”

The dial emitted a soft chime, the sort of sound a glass makes when heat finds its crack. Both of them looked down. The needle had drifted 0.3 degrees west of their last mark.

Sona’s brows lifted. “It’s asking a question.”

Jian chewed the inside of his cheek. “The question is whether we’re brave enough to be wrong.”

She extended her wrist, exposing the thin membrane of the interface patch. “Sync your pulse to mine. If our HRV diverges more than five milliseconds, the route voids. No retries.”

He hesitated only a second, then pressed his own patch against hers. The soft click of the magnetic latch sounded ridiculously intimate. A green dot flickered, then steadied. Their heartbeats overlapped on the tiny shared display—hers slightly faster, his arrhythmic after the climb.

“Your heart’s doing a tango,” she observed.

“Your heart’s over-caffeinated,” he countered.

They watched the waveform braid and unbraid, a green thread stitching two bodies into one provisional organism. He felt the moment their autonomic systems negotiated—like two rivers learning to share a bed. His shoulders loosened without permission.

Sona’s voice barely rose above the ventricular hush. “Memory’s tongue or glacier’s confession?”

He thought of the candidate’s last breath fogging the visor, the way the word *sorry* had condensed into a droplet and slid sideways. He thought of the moonlight outside, cold enough to burn skin off illusion. Then he thought of the rectangle of night air waiting at the corridor’s end—how it would feel to step through without the weight of a ghost riding his back.

He drew a slow breath through his teeth, tasting both forks at once.
