Jian watched the filament calm between Corv’s fingers, its black-gold shimmer settling into a steady pulse that mirrored the slow beat of the Emperor’s borrowed heart.  
“Clock’s still running,” he said. “We’re holding open a gate that wasn’t designed for more than one soul.”  
“Speak for yourself,” Gideon muttered, sliding the Klei ring into a pocket that swallowed it without a seam. “I’m at least half a soul on Tuesdays.”  
Corv exhaled through his teeth. “Three minutes before the splice starts digesting the decoy signature. After that it’ll ask inconvenient questions about why dreamers bleed coffee.”  
Sona tilted her head, listening to the sub-audible lull between lattice nodes. “We can gift it a lullaby. Or we can fold the question back into a loop of self-reference so tight it forgets it asked.”  
“Philosophy as denial-of-service attack,” Jian said. “I approve, but we still have to walk out.”  
Corv turned the Yìshí again, letting the bead click once against the pearl. “The Emperor’s endocrine is a cathedral bell. Every beat makes the nave ring. We leave a dampener or the dream wakes up screaming.”  
“Dampener costs us half the salvage,” Gideon said. “Committee wants the cortisol imprint intact.”  
“Committee can bite me,” Corv replied. “Sona, can you thread a capacitor into the oxytocin receipt? Delay the crash without erasing the echo?”  
She gave a small, wicked smile. “I can tuck a tiny lie in the aftertaste. Imagine the summer rain arriving five seconds late—just enough to miss the memory bus.”  
Jian’s cuff counted down: one-hundred-eighty seconds.  
Gideon eyed the Gardener’s distant silhouette. “That thing’s pruning the same strand twice. Either it’s senile or it’s stalling for us.”  
“Don’t anthropomorphize the maintenance daemon,” Sona said. “It will take it as consent.”  
Corv snorted. “We already consented the minute we signed the waiver in the infirmary roof font.”  
“Tiles like busted piano keys,” Gideon echoed softly, testing whether nostalgia still hurt.  
Jian’s mouth twitched. “You two and your rooftop cult. Some of us read the fine print.”  
“Some of us were busy vomiting vertigo,” Corv said. “I remember the peach you never delivered.”  
“Consider it accruing interest,” Gideon said. “Compounded by altitude sickness.”  
Sona raised a hand. “If we’re done flirting with the past, can we decide? Capacitor or dampener?”  
Corv met her gaze. “Capacitor. Keep the ghost, lose the scream.”  
“Consensus?” Jian asked.  
Gideon flicked an imaginary coin. “Heads we lie, tails we lie prettier. Capacitor wins.”  
Sona knelt, pressing two fingers to the lattice floor until a bead of liquid copper rose like a tear. She coaxed it onto the filament’s underside where the pearl had kissed. The copper bead flattened, spreading into a thin veil that caught the green flare and held it a fraction longer than physics allowed.  
“Receipt rewritten,” she said. “Delay is five seconds plus or minus one synaptic jitter.”  
Corv watched the veil settle, then looked at Jian. “We still need an exit cue. The Gardener’s shears know our scent.”  
Jian tapped his cuff. “I can spoof a maintenance heartbeat. Broadcast a false all-clear every eleven seconds. Loop lasts ninety seconds before the splice notices the pattern.”  
“Do it,” Corv said.  
Jian exhaled, steady and slow, then subvocalized the command. A low thrummed chord—felt more than heard—rolled outward through the lattice. Far away, the Gardener paused again, shears half open. A filament it had already trimmed curved back toward the blade, confused but obedient.  
Gideon lifted an eyebrow. “You’re playing shepherd to a blind god. Hope your lullaby holds.”  
“Hope is a hormone,” Jian said. “I brought extra.”  
Corv felt the tether around his wrist slacken another degree. The pearl grew lighter, as if the Emperor’s dream had decided to carry its own weight for once.  
“Thirty seconds,” Jian warned. “We walk single file, no sudden metaphors.”  
Gideon chuckled. “I’ll save my metaphors for dessert.”  
Sona rose, brushing imaginary dust from her knees. “Ghost of rain is locked and loaded. On my mark, we disengage.”  
Corv looked once more at the Gardener. It had resumed its quiet pruning, but slower—each cut deliberate, almost tender. He wondered if the daemon felt the counterfeit heartbeat like a child feels a bedtime story: safe enough to close its eyes.  
“Mark,” Sona said.  
Corv released the Yìshí. The jade bead rolled off his thumb and vanished into the lattice without sound.  
The filament dimmed to a soft afterglow. The pearl cooled against his palm, no longer alive but not yet artifact.  
They stepped backward—one pace, two—along the narrow strip of stable code Jian had mapped. The lattice rippled once, sighed, and stilled.  
Behind them, the Gardener lifted another frayed filament, cradled it like a sleeping child, and began the careful work of persuading it to forget the wound had ever been its own.
