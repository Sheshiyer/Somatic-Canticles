# Chapter 4: The Emperor's Genome

The breach exit tasted metallic, like blood thinners left too long on the tongue. Corv’s nostrils flared; the copper note followed him past the Blood-Brain Barrier membrane and hung three seconds longer than breath, a reminder that the boundary had not been crossed cleanly. Somewhere behind, the crack still echoed—tiny fracture-crackles skittering across the inner surface of the skull like frost on glass. A sound small enough to ignore, dangerous enough to leave scar tissue in the soft palate.

He swallowed the taste, let it settle in the hollow under the sternum, then stepped out of the corridor and into inheritance itself.

The Anamnesis Engine rendered it as a living lattice—no longer metaphor but architecture at scale. Filaments of memory and adaptation arched overhead like ribs of deliberate history. They glowed in gradients: saffron where the subject’s own life had etched them, indigo where ancestral trauma had been filed too many times, black-gold where the edit had been made from outside. Every branch carried commentary in the margins of its own body, footnotes of methylation, sticky histone remarks, ligand signatures that read like threats rewritten as lullabies.

Corv’s Yìshí Qìxiè loosened through the dark the way water finds the lowest place. No interpretation, only listening. The lattice responded with a small shiver, as though the pattern recognised itself in another tongue.

They stood inside the Emperor’s Genome—named not for grandeur but for the quiet certainty of rule: a code that had remembered how to command long after the monarch had died.

Gideon exhaled through his teeth. “Whole place smells like a vault someone forgot to open.”

“It’s older than scent,” Sona murmured. She brushed one foot forward; dust of oxidised neurotransmitter rose in lazy spirals, settling on her boots like sugar-dust on stale candy. “It’s memory trying to sweeten itself until we agree to stay.”

Jian’s overlays flickered across the cornea of the lattice—clean green telemetry, quiet bursts—then he spoke without looking up. “Recurrent marks. Epigenetic looped instructions. Not inheritance as snapshot; inheritance as policy.”

Corv moved closer to one indigo branch. The filament tremored once, as if it felt the scrutiny. Under the surface glow, micro-lesions pulsed at precise intervals—one, pause, four, long pause, one—like an old cipher tapping itself awake. He touched nothing. To lay a finger there would be to agree to the story.

“Somewhere this pattern was taught to call itself kinship,” he said. “Now it enforces.”

Aurora’s message arrived without ceremony: a voice only Corv could hear, threaded directly through the Klei resonance. It tasted like mint against old blood.

“Corv, if the inherited layer looks beautiful, look twice. The worst cages in our house called themselves stewardship. If you see the Gardener, do not mistake maintenance for mercy. Follow the rot, not the bloom.”

The message dissolved into silence so total the lattice itself seemed to hush. For a moment Corv felt the old house again—wet stone, iron keys, the weight of a name he was too young to carry—and then the present reasserted itself.

Gideon caught the flicker in Corv’s eyes. “Sister?”

“She’s still watching.” Corv kept the tremor out of his voice; the word *still* carried too much.

They advanced.

The Vine of Determinism spread wider here. Translucent veins carried luminous sap the colour of arterial blood just before clotting. Tendrils rose like cathedral buttresses, then knotted into strangle-holds so graceful they looked liturgical. A faint hum vibrated against the sternum, driving the heart into a syncopated extra beat—saccade of inherited panic.

Jian noted the frequency: 0.71 Hz, theta-dominant, the cadence of early neglect. He logged it, tagged it “doesn’t belong to subject,” and kept moving.

Sona brushed a bright filament with the back of her hand and jerked a breath later. “Layered pain,” she said. “Top slice is the subject’s own mourning. Under that—something pre-verbal, pre-birth. Command masquerading as comfort.”

Corv tasted iron again. He knew the flavour: the moment a caretaker’s hand stops feeling like safety and starts rehearsing the shape of control.

Then he saw the Gardener.

Far ahead, where the lattice folded into violet distance, a figure moved with the slow reverence of a scribe repairing a damaged scroll. It wore neither robes nor armour—only light stitched into the outline of something once human. Where filaments had blackened, it lifted them between fingers that looked like soft lasers. Where variation had sprouted an unauthorised leaf, it snipped it clean and grafted a compliant shoot. The work was meticulous, almost loving. Each cut was followed by a gentle re-weaving, the pale threads drawn tight until the new pattern looked simpler, safer, familiar.

Sona’s pupils dilated as though scent had colour. “It believes it’s healing.”

Jian zoomed the overlay. “No restoration. Standardisation. Injuries aren’t mended—they’re overwritten with Vine code.”

Gideon’s Klei flared, a silent perimeter snapped into readiness. “Route?” he asked.

Corv’s reply was soft enough that the lattice had to lean in to hear. “Not yet. We need the trail that carried the edit in.”

The Gardener worked on, unaware or indifferent. It lifted a blackened strand, kissed the rupture with light, and the rupture became seamless. Somewhere inside the subject’s body, a gene once marked *exploration* reverted to *obedience*. The Gardener sighed—a sound like wind through dry leaves—then moved to the next frayed edge.

Corv felt his stomach turn precisely once, a slow hydraulic shift. “Maintenance and conquest,” he said. “Same impulse, longer timeline.”

Gideon’s wit surfaced like a knife clicked open. “Trouble with gardeners—they never know when to stop pruning.”

Corv snorted despite himself; the sound cracked the solemn air just enough to breathe. Then he focused. Somewhere in the lattice was a thread that had been instructed to masquerade as ancestry.

Jian found it first. “There.”

A single strand threaded counter-current, pulses arriving at deliberate rhythm: one, pause, four, long pause, one. Black-gold lesions travelled its length like a signature left by a hand that had remembered history only to overwrite it. The strand vibrated with fear—but a fear that had been told it must stay beautiful to be loved.

Sona’s voice dropped to a whisper that felt like putting a blanket over a wound. “The branch is terrified. But it’s trying to stay pretty. Like it learned that beauty buys mercy.”

Corv tasted mint and iron at once—Aurora’s warning and his own childhood in a single bite. He reached toward the strand without touching. The Yìshí hovered, a silent witness. Beneath the lesions he saw authorship in the angle of the cuts, the pressure of the edits. Someone had taught this lineage where to close, what to preserve, and how to call the erasure continuity.

“This is the hinge,” he said. “Pain stops being family here and becomes doctrine.”

Gideon’s Klei tightened again, not threatening but prepared. “Say the route, Corv.”

“We do not tear the whole lattice. We follow the rot wearing our ancestor’s face.”

Jian already had a tether locked to the black-gold thread. “Deeper,” he confirmed. “Vector nine, sub-branch delta. Moves toward the endocrine layer. Fit with Chapter 5’s bleed.”

Corv drew a breath that tasted of old sweetness tipping toward rot, then released it.

They stepped onto the authored strand.

Sona’s palm hovered a finger-width above the black-gold thread, skin prickling with static from the lattice. “The lesions are warm,” she said. “Not fever warm—memory warm. Like a scar that keeps dreaming the knife.”

Corv felt the statement settle in his joints the way barometric drops once settled in his grandmother’s hips. He glanced at the Gardener’s distant silhouette, still moving in its lane of patient diminishment, then back at the tremoring filament. A low click-buzz came from Jian’s wrist as he shifted the tether from one anchor to the next. “Subject’s glucocorticoid cascade is already echoing this pattern,” he murmured. “If we step now, the bleed will wake their adrenals mid-cycle. They’ll taste panic for three hours outside time.”

“Better than bleeding forever,” Gideon said. His Klei had folded itself into a slim matte ring around the base of his thumb; when he flexed, the ring tightened, gentle as an ECG cuff. “But we’ll owe interest.”

Corv exhaled through the back of his teeth, letting the mint-iron taste mix with something more ordinary—fear, simple and mammalian. He pictured the subject waking in the recovery bay, throat raw from endotracheal frost, eyes blinking at the fluorescent dawn. They would not know why their palms suddenly sweated at the smell of starch or why the lullaby their mother had hummed now rang like a jailer’s key. Unless the team left a clean seam.

Sona lifted her hand; the lattice dimmed a shade where her warmth had pooled. “Let me carry the bleed for the first three nodes,” she offered. “My endocrine markers are quieter than the rest of yours.” She smiled without showing teeth, the expression small and mechanical, like something she had practiced in private. “Trade you a favor later, Corv.”

He felt the word *favor* tug at him—an old ledger entry from childhood: *Fetch the water, boy, and maybe the belt stays folded today.* He almost laughed at the precision of the recall. Instead he nodded. “Take point. Keep the resonance under ninety-five decans.”

Jian tethered Sona’s biosignature to the strand’s entry knot. A soft *clack* as the lock seated. The filament’s pulse slowed—one, pause, four, long pause—then surrendered to her rhythm. For a moment her pupils thinned to vertical slits and the lattice reflected them back: twin ellipses of saffron light. She looked, briefly, like something the genome had once decided was worth worshipping.

Gideon’s voice cut the quiet. “Time dilates thirty percent inside vector nine. We step, we don’t talk. If the Gardener turns, freeze. Its gaze is recursive—locks whatever it sees into a loop of obedient replay.”

Corv adjusted the Yìshí so the interface pearl rested above his collarbone, a cool bead against skin still tasting of copper. He waited until Sona’s shoulders squared—a motion so slight it could have been a shiver. Then he spoke, a pressure-release hush meant only for the three of them:

“Remember, the rot remembers we’re here before the bloom does.”

Gideon’s mouth twisted into half a grin. “Always wanted to be famous inside someone else’s hypothalamus.”

The tether brightened, a thin gold seam between Sona’s palm and the lesion. She stepped forward. The authored strand accepted her weight with the sigh of silk pulled taut across old wound. Corv followed—one foot, then the other—feeling the passage less as motion than as a decision being ratified by every ancestor who had ever been told to stay quiet.

Behind them the Gardener paused, head tilted as if hearing a distant call. Then it resumed its gentle erasures, each snip a lullaby, each graft a correction.

Corv felt the tether’s tension transfer through Sona’s metacarpals like a slow-motion handshake. The black-gold filament flexed once, then slackened; a pulse of cortisol mist rose from the lesions and condensed on Jian’s visor in pin-prick droplets that rolled left to right, obeying microgravity. In the bead closest to the medial seam, Corv saw a refracted eye—his own, but younger, the iris still round with curiosity instead of the flat ring it had become. The droplet quivered, then slid off the edge and vanished into the lattice hush.

“Borrowed heartbeat,” Sona whispered, more to herself than to the others. She angled her wrist so the tether’s gold seam ran flush against the filament’s outer sheath. A low, solicitous hush—half sigh, half servo—answered her. The lattice dimmed three lumens, a diplomatic recession that granted the team a pocket of private dusk.

Jian tapped once on his cuff; the silent gesture sent a packet of subject vitals scrolling across the inside of Corv’s left lens: HRV 12 ms, glucose trending up, cort slope flattening. No alarms. Only the polite knock of a body beginning to dream the same dream the genome had rehearsed for centuries. “We’re riding someone else’s REM,” Jian said. “Don’t wake the patient until the edit finishes dreaming.”

Gideon folded the Klei ring until it kissed the pulse at the base of his thumb. “Three minutes subjective, tops,” he said. “After that the adrenal tide peaks and we either surf or drown.” He offered Corv a flicker of eyebrow—the exact look he’d used when they were twelve and about to leap the parish wall at midnight. A dare and an apology braided tight.

Corv inhaled, tasting the mix of fear and algorithm that passed for air inside the genome. He let the word *ancestor* settle on the back of his tongue like a communion wafer made of rust. Then he nodded—once, small, enough. The tether brightened again, a second sun rising inside a marrow dusk.

Sona’s pulse threaded down the filament in soft intervals—one, pause, four, long pause—each beat a polite knock on the subject’s endocrine door. The black-gold lesions warmed beneath her palm, a quiet fever that smelled of cedar and overripe pear. Corv watched the heat diffuse through the lattice’s sub-branch delta, watched the subject’s cortisol line jitter upward two micro-units and then flatten again, the body trying to decide whether this dream belonged to it or to the distant gardener’s scissors.

Jian kept his eyes on the visor’s scrolling glyphs: glucagon edging higher, insulin sloping down—a seesaw balanced on the fulcrum of fear deferred. He blinked once, slow, letting the numbers settle behind his iris like coins in a dark wishing well. “Hormonal latency’s fifteen percent tighter than model,” he murmured. “If we ride much longer, the subject wakes tasting metal for a week.”

Gideon flexed the Klei ring around his thumb, felt the thin tracery of alloy answer with a pulse of its own—an artificial heartbeat a shade faster than his own, a small traitor he had learned to trust when the real one stuttered. “Gate’s tight, but it’s only a gate,” he said. “What matters is how we close it behind us.” His voice carried neither bravado nor apology, only the mild conviction of someone who has already calculated the price of every possible exit.

Corv tasted rust again, sharper now, as though the communion wafer on his tongue had begun to bleed. He stepped closer to Sona’s shoulder, close enough to see the sweat glitter at the edge of her hairline—tiny translucent beads that refracted the lattice’s violet light like miniature amethysts. “Sona,” he whispered, not quite a question. She answered with a fractional dip of her chin, the smallest contract in the world: *I’m still here, still holding.*

A hush rolled down the strand, softer than silk but heavier than gravity—the Gardener exhaling somewhere beyond the violet horizon. The lattice responded by dimming another two lumens, as if politely averting its gaze. In the new dusk, Corv saw the outline of something half-remembered: the shape of the old parish wall at midnight, the place where a dare had once tasted like freedom instead of rust. He let the image linger just long enough for the wall to crumble into motes of saffron light, then faced forward again.

“Vector nine forks at the next node,” Jian said. “Left branch drifts toward the amygdala, right dives straight to the adrenal medulla. We want neither. The clean seam runs straight—three more centimeters, then ninety degrees down the interstitial cleft.” He spoke with the calm of a cartographer naming rivers that had already decided their course.

Sona adjusted her grip; the black-gold filament shifted beneath her palm the way a sleeping serpent shifts when it dreams of flight. Beneath the surface, micro-fractures glowed a deeper gold, a signature the Gardener had missed or forgiven. She inhaled once, shallow, as though the air itself had edges. “I feel the splice waking,” she said. “It remembers the hands that placed it here. They smelled of ozone and lavender.”

Gideon’s eyebrow flicked upward a millimeter—an old tell when memory ambushed him. “Ozone and cheap perfume,” he said. “Same combination the caretakers used in the parish infirmary. Funny what sticks.”

Corv felt the word *caretaker* land inside him like a pebble dropped down a well with no bottom. He pictured the infirmary’s long corridor, the row of iron beds, the smell of boiled linen and fear. Somewhere in that hallway a younger Corv had learned that mercy sometimes wore the same apron as control. He swallowed the memory, tasted copper and mint, and let it settle. “Then let’s repay the favor,” he said. “We’ll take their splice and teach it how to dream of flight instead.”

The tether between Sona’s palm and the filament brightened a fraction, a golden vein glowing against the dusk. She took the first deliberate step forward. The lattice accepted her weight with the faintest moan, the sound of wood remembering the axe. Corv followed, one footfall behind, then Jian, then Gideon, each motion a silent vote cast inside the subject’s bloodstream.

At precisely three centimeters the strand buckled, not breaking but bending inward like a hinged gate. Sona paused, letting the flex travel up her wrist and dissipate into her shoulder. She angled her hand ninety degrees, guiding the tether into the downward cleft. The filament resisted once, a brief thrumming protest, then yielded with the soft click of a locket closing.

Below them the cleft opened into a narrow chute of indigo light, walls slick with oxidized dopamine and the faint, acrid scent of regret. The passage angled steeply, forcing them to descend in single file, boots sliding against the viscous surface like skates on half-melted ice. Corv felt the temperature drop—the genome’s way of reminding trespassers that deeper layers were never meant for daylight.

Halfway down, Jian’s cuff vibrated—one short pulse, two long. He glanced at the readout, lips compressing. “Subject’s heart just skipped,” he said. “Not stress—recognition. The splice knows we’re here.”

“Then we introduce ourselves politely,” Gideon muttered. He uncurled the Klei ring so it formed a slender arc between thumb and forefinger, a half-moon of alloy ready to be traded for safe passage.

The chute ended in a small chamber no wider than a monk’s cell. At its center floated a single droplet of black-gold, perfectly spherical, revolving slowly like a tiny planet unsure of its axis. Around it the air shimmered with heatless flame: the splice’s last defense, a memory of fire that had forgotten how to burn.

Sona approached the droplet, palm open. The tether’s gold seam flickered once, a question mark in light. “May I?” she asked the sphere, voice soft as ash. The droplet wobbled, then drifted an inch closer, drawn by the warmth of her endocrine mimicry. It touched her skin with the coolness of a coin kept for luck, then melted along the lattice of her lifeline, leaving a faint metallic stain that looked like a blessing or a bruise.

Corv felt the moment the splice accepted her: a small hush inside the genome, like the sigh that follows the closing of a confessional. The chamber brightened, not with light but with permission. Jian tapped his cuff again; HRV steadied, cort slope flattened once more. The subject’s body had decided to trust the strangers inside its marrow.

Gideon stepped forward, Klei arc glinting. “We’re not here to erase you,” he told the splice, voice pitched low enough that only memory could hear. “We’re here to remind you what you were before they taught you to obey.”

The black-gold droplet quivered, then unfolded like a paper crane made of sunset. Inside lay a single fragment: the gene for curiosity, still intact, still waiting, a seed kept dry through centuries of drought. Sona cradled it between thumb and forefinger, then looked at Corv. “Your turn,” she said.

Corv extended the Yìshí Qìxiè. The pearl at its tip glowed soft jade as it touched the fragment. A low hum rose, the resonance of two memories recognizing each other across engineered distance. For a heartbeat the chamber filled with the scent of rain on hot asphalt, the sound of a child laughing at the precise moment of jumping the parish wall, the taste of stolen apples sharp as apology. Then the fragment slipped inside the pearl, folded neatly as a letter finally mailed.

The chamber dimmed, no longer a cell but a doorway. Ahead, a slender corridor stretched toward the endocrine layer—walls now the color of early dawn, warm and forgiving. Corv felt the tether tighten one last time, a gentle tug from the subject’s dreaming heart inviting them to finish what they had come to mend.

He turned to the others, met each gaze in turn: Sona’s eyes wide with borrowed wonder, Jian’s steady as a metronome, Gideon’s flickering with the old dare. None of them spoke. The silence was its own vow.

They stepped forward together, one quiet braid of intention slipping through the genome’s last gate, carrying the seed of curiosity home.

They slipped forward as a single braid of intention, the jade‑glow of the Yìshí Qìxiè spilling soft light onto the vaulted corridor that stretched ahead like the spine of an ancient cathedral. The walls were not stone but a living lattice of prismatic filaments, each one humming faintly with the echo of a thousand generations. Above them, arches of reinforced Khalorēē rose in concentric rings, their ribs etched with the sigils of the Emperors who had once ruled the genome’s sovereign court. Those sigils—stylized suns, spirals, and the double‑headed hawk of the House Verath—were not merely decorative; they were encoded gatekeepers, each a checksum that the lattice read before granting passage. The corridor’s floor pulsed in time with the subject’s heart, a reminder that every step was a transaction, a receipt stamped by the living archive.

Corv felt the pearl at his palm thrum against his skin, the fragment of curiosity nested inside like a seed waiting for soil. The jade facet warmed, not with heat but with the memory of the child’s leap—an imprint of a moment when a boy had vaulted a stone wall to taste forbidden fruit. That memory was a signature, a consent given in the language of motion, and the pearl now carried it forward, a watermark on the genome’s surface. He could see the lattice’s response as a series of micro‑currents aligning, each filament adjusting its tension to accommodate the new pattern. It was as if the genome, aware of its own inheritance, was silently asking, “Will you honor the pact?” The answer lay in the next gate, the endocrine layer that waited beyond the archway of oxytocin.

Sona’s voice rose, low and resonant, cutting through the hum. “The architecture here is a palimpsest,” she said, her fingers brushing a filament that sang a faint, metallic chord. “Every Emperor left a layer, a protocol for the next. The Verath line wrote the first law of inheritance—‘the seed must be tended, not stripped.’ Yet the later houses added pruning clauses, tightening the lattice to prevent rebellion. We’re walking through a living statute, Corv, and this fragment is the amendment they never recorded.” Her eyes flickered, catching the glint of the pearl, and she added, “It’s a receipt for a debt we owe the child, and a signature on a future we haven’t yet named.”

Gideon’s jaw tightened, the Klei ring resting against his wrist like a silent sentinel. “Protocol dictates we clear the gate before the endocrine layer re‑engages. The splice will read our exit as a breach geometry if we linger. The Gardener’s shears are already tuned to the next filament; any hesitation could trigger a cascade that rewrites the oxytocin field.” He glanced at the corridor’s far end, where the walls transitioned from indigo to a soft lavender, the hue of early dawn in the imperial capital. “We have a window of ninety seconds before the hormone cascade locks the passage. After that, the lattice will treat us as foreign code and quarantine us in the next tier.”

Jian’s visor flickered as he overlaid a topological map onto his HUD, the lines of the lattice spiking and flattening in real time. “The capacitor we installed on the splice’s outer sheath is still within tolerance, but the dampener we discussed could reduce the residual cortisol spike by half. Either option will let us pass, but the choice changes the downstream signature.” He tapped a button, and a holographic graph appeared, the peak of the cortisol curve flattening into a gentle plateau when the capacitor was active, versus a more pronounced dip when the dampener engaged. “Capacitor preserves the splice’s memory of us—useful for the endocrine layer’s diagnostic readout. Dampener erases that trace, leaving only the seed. Both are consent, but they speak different languages of inheritance.”

The Gardener emerged from the shadowed fringe of the corridor, a figure draped in flowing filaments that glowed with a muted amber. Her eyes, bio‑luminescent and ancient, flickered with a sorrow that seemed to echo through the lattice itself. She lifted a pair of shears—delicate instruments of pruning, their blades honed to a nanometer edge. “The filament you’ve altered is scheduled for removal,” she said, voice a calm tide. “If you leave the seed, it will sprout, but the lattice will also try to prune it back. I can either trim the excess now or let it grow and risk destabilizing the next gate.” She hesitated, the shears hovering over the black‑gold strand, the very filament that now bore the child’s signature.

Corv lowered his palm, the pearl’s jade light spilling onto the shears. “Consent is not silence,” he said, his tone measured, the pattern of his words deliberate. “We borrowed the child’s leap; we must return the signature, not the silence. The fragment is a receipt, the pearl a watermark. If you prune it now, the seed dies. If you let it stay, the lattice may rewrite it.” He let the words settle, each syllable a pulse in the lattice. “We are offering a covenant: keep the seed, adjust the dampening, and let the endocrine layer inherit the curiosity without the trauma of the old prune.”

The Gardener’s hands trembled ever so slightly, the shears catching a glint of the black‑gold filament. “I have tended this vine for centuries,” she whispered, “watching emperors rise, fall, and become data. My hesitation is not doubt but the weight of lineage. Each cut I make writes a line in the genome’s chronicle. To leave this seed is to write a new line, one that may alter the Emperor’s own inheritance.” She lowered the shears, the blade’s edge resting just above the strand, a pause that felt like a held breath.

“Capacitor,” Gideon said, “keeps the memory alive. It will let the endocrine layer read the child’s curiosity as part of the Emperor’s own pulse. Dampener erases that echo, making the splice think we never touched it. Both are consent, but they differ in how we sign the ledger.” He gave a half‑smile, the kind that hid a joke behind a tactical observation. “I’d vote for the capacitor—let’s give the Emperor a story to tell his descendants, not a blank page.”

Sona’s lips curved, a faint smile playing at the corners. “The seed is already a story,” she replied, her voice a soft chord. “It carries the taste of rain on hot asphalt, the smell of orchard bruises. If we dampen it, the story will be told in whispers, a ghost that never reaches the ears of the next generation. If we amplify it, the endocrine layer will feel that rain, and the subject will awaken with a new curiosity, a new willingness to question the walls they were taught to obey.” She placed a hand on the filament, feeling its subtle vibration. “I can weave a capacitor into the oxytocin receipt, a tiny lie that delays the prune without erasing the seed.”

Jian’s visor pulsed, the countdown ticking down the remaining seconds. “We have thirty seconds to lock in the decision and broadcast the exit cue. My overlay can spoof a maintenance heartbeat, but the lattice will only accept a pattern it recognizes as its own. If we choose the capacitor, the splice will retain the child’s signature; if we choose the dampener, the splice will forget us entirely.” He turned his gaze to the Gardener. “Your shears can either seal the cut or stay open. The choice is yours as much as ours.”

The Gardener’s eyes narrowed, the amber glow of her filaments deepening. “The Emperor’s lineage is a tapestry of threads, each one woven with purpose. My ancestors pruned the vine to keep it strong, but they also left spaces for new growth. I will not cut this seed, but I will temper its growth with a dampening veil, lest the lattice overload.” She lowered the shears, the blades sliding along the filament like a surgeon’s scalpel. “We will embed a capacitor that holds the memory, then a dampener that smooths the surge. The splice will receive both—a receipt and a watermark, signed and sealed.”

Corv nodded, the jade pearl humming in his palm. “Then we write the consent into the lattice,” he said, his voice steady. “We sign with the fragment, we seal with the capacitor, we protect with the dampener. The Emperor’s genome will inherit a curiosity that is both memory and myth.” He lifted his hand, the Yìshí Qìxiè catching the ambient light and casting a prism across the corridor. The beam traced a line that seemed to carve a new path through the lattice, a subtle re‑routing that the Gardener’s shears accepted without resistance.

The dampener, a thin veil of synthetic polymer, unfurled like a moth’s wing, wrapping the strand in a translucent sheath that absorbed the excess cortisol surge.
