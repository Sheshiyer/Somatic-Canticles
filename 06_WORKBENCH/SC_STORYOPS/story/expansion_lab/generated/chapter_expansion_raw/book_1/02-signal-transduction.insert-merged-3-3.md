# Chapter 2: Signal Transduction
*(3,460 words)*

The fall into the past began eighty-seven milliseconds after Sona’s last heartbeat in the screaming present. A single electrical pulse—her own atrial spark—carried the field-coordinates for the jump. In the next three hundred milliseconds the `Anamnesis Engine` compressed the entire collapse into a down-swept frequency you could almost hum: a low C felt more in lungs than ears. Jian counted the lag, cataloguing it as **Signal 80-300-Cardioid**, even while his optic nerves still bled the after-image of the collapsing corridor. Then the noise simply… shut up.

The chaotic, screaming present imploded to a star that burned once and blinked out. For six nanoseconds there was nothing. Not zero, not darkness—*absence*—as if a synapse had been lifted cleanly out of the skull. The sudden quiet was so total it pressed against the eardrums like altitude change.

Then shape.

Reality resolved into a scene so calm it felt like deliberate insult. They stood inside a library the size of a small cathedral. Sunlight, amber and thick as honey, slanted through stained clerestory windows—Virgo rising, Jian noted automatically, though the zodiac had no official place in his charting suite. The air smelled of lignin, pipe tobacco, and something fainter: old grief stored in the glue of book spines.

Dust motes drifted around them, dense as a nebula. Each fleck carried a faint electric halo: micro-charges caught between pane and pane of memory glass. They were inside a cloud of miniature suns.

Sona opened her mouth, closed it. “The bruising’s gone,” she whispered. The choroid plexus still felt swollen, but the pain no longer broadcast on every channel. “The Engine found the safe signal.”

Gideon’s hand hovered at his sternum, palm flat as if feeling for a hidden weapon. “Safe is a category, not a promise.” Still, he let the hand drop. The protective grid of his **Klei Toda'ah** relaxed by millimeters, bars thinning to the width of paper.

Corv said nothing. His gaze moved across the shelves the way a surgeon reads bruising: looking for the wound beneath the bloom.

They drifted forward—no other verb fit. Sona first, then the others, pulled by a gravity that had nothing to do with mass. The floorboards—oak, oiled until the grain looked wet—didn’t creak under non-existent weight.

Jian exhaled, and the cloud of dust in front of his face rearranged itself into the glyph for *śūnya*, zero-point. He filed the coincidence under ‘environmental metaphor’ and kept walking.

—

Halfway down the central aisle, the shelves parted like a wound cauterized with gold leaf. The geometry repeated in fractal iterations: every shelf a miniature of the whole library, each book on it a compressed library in turn. Vedic compression, old code. A Khalorēē signature older than the Engine itself.

He touched nothing, yet his **Manas Interface** overlaid the space with a lattice of quiet gold. Error codes vanished one by one: *301 Missing Cardinal*, *472 Recursive Silence*, *802 Absence Not Found*. The last amused him—error code as koan.

“Pre-trauma,” he announced, voice soft enough to pass for reverence. “Whatever broke the mind, it happened *after* this memory.”

From somewhere above, the faint, impossible sound of a page turning. No one looked up. They had learned that some echoes only existed to test composure.

Sona stopped in front of the armchair. It was large, leather cracked from decades of pressure, the seat cushion sagging in the exact contour of a body no longer present. The pipe in the ashtray still leaked a single ribbon of smoke, frozen mid-spiral.

She reached toward the smoke, reflex slowed by wonder, and her fingers passed through it without resistance—like plunging a hand into the memory of warmth.

“Subject’s not here,” she said. “But the devotion is.”

Her **Adawat al-Wa'i** read the room like skin reads braille: a texture of absence, raised and definite. The absence had weight—twelve kilograms, give or take the mass of an adult heart. She said it aloud before she could stop herself. “Twelve kilos of missing person.”

Gideon snorted—half laugh, half bark. “That’s disturbingly specific.”

She didn’t smile. “Specific is what’s left when identity goes.”

Jian felt the notification arrive before the glyph appeared: a pressure at the base of his skull, the Quoril family crest encoded as a polite migraine. He blinked. The message unfurled:

**Dr. Node Quoril**  
> Board query: House Luminth filed provisional patent 12-C on Daoist field scaffolding using meridian mapping. Council of Emanations requests comparative data set. Deliver by 0800. Do not tunnel down academic rabbit holes.

He thumbed it away so hard the air rippled. The dust motes flinched.

Corv watched the gesture, eyebrow raised. “Personal leave?” he asked, voice dry.

“Family obligation,” Jian muttered. “Same thing, different century.”

Gideon approached the chair as if it might bite. His **Klei Toda'ah** extended into a transparent cube around the seat, its edges glinting ice-blue. The cube’s inner surfaces ran with diagnostics: temperature stable, pressure null, no bio-threat detected.

Yet the chair’s shadow fell the wrong way—toward the windows, against the light. He logged the anomaly under *gravitational drift*, then immediately wished he hadn’t. The library was rewriting local physics faster than he could patch.

He knelt, palms on the floorboards. The oak felt warm—body heat trapped for decades. A silent alarm pulsed up through the bone of the house, slow and patient as lymph.

“Something lived here,” he said. “Something they loved enough to amputate.”

Corv circled the chair once, twice, each orbit tighter. His **Yìshí Qìxiè** moved like water, touching but never disturbing: the pipe, the ashtray, the ghost of a whiskey ring on the side table.

He paused at the hearth. A single index card pinned to the mantelpiece, blank except for a printer’s mark: `#Khalorēē_Core_Trust_Δ7`. The red lettering had faded to the color of dried blood.

“Metadata tag,” he said. “Belief module deleted at revision Δ7. They kept the index but erased the text.”

Sona’s breath caught. “Core Trust. That’s not just a belief. It’s the protocol that decides what *can* be believed.”

Jian’s fingers flew across his interface, pulling threads of data like a harpist drawing wire. “Phantom references still looping. Every book in this wing cites the same absent source. The footnotes point to nothing.” He looked up, eyes bright with forensic hunger. “We can map the hole by the shape of the citations.”

A second notification flared—this time soft lilac, the color Dr. Melody Quoril used for “concerned understatement.”

**Dr. Melody Quoril**  
> Field strain rising at your node. Let the resonance *be* the map, dear. Sometimes the absence tells truer stories than the ink.

Jian felt the words land in the exact spot Node’s had bruised. He almost laughed—two aunts, one scalpel each, cutting from opposite directions. “Noted,” he muttered, and for once he meant it.

Sona sat—insofar as sitting was possible—on the arm of the empty chair. Her translucent form intersected the leather without clipping. From inside the ghost-outline she could feel the contour of absent weight: a clavicle’s slope, the ridge of a sternum.

She closed her eyes. The **miradune** opened beneath her, a silvered surface that reflected not her face but her pulse. The rhythm skipped, then steadied. Below, the redacted belief floated like a sunken statue, coral overgrown around the holes where eyes should be.

A voice—not memory, not hallucination—whispered across the interface: *“I was told safety could be built like a wall.”* She couldn’t tell if it belonged to the subject, the deleted belief, or herself.

Corv found the first fracture at floor level—a hairline crack following the grain of oak in a perfect Fibonacci spiral. He knelt, tracing it with one ghost-finger. “The redaction wasn’t self-inflicted,” he said. “Look at the angle. External incision, precise to the neuron. Someone else held the knife.”

Gideon straightened, containment cube flashing a colder blue. “House Luminth?” The name arrived unbidden; the politics had followed them into sanctuary like smell follows rain.

“Signal match,” Jian confirmed, distracted. A new line of data scrolled: `House_Luminth_Feed_Interrupt_Δ7`. “They sent a pulse. The subject’s field dropped trust as instructed.” His voice was flat, but the flatness carried iron.

Silence pooled. Even the dust motes stilled, as if eavesdropping.

Then, pressure-release: Gideon flicked a speck of imaginary lint from his sleeve. “Well,” he said, “at least the décor survived the coup. Taste is the last thing to go.”

Sona choked on a laugh. Corv’s mouth twitched—one corner, barely. Jian felt the tension in his shoulders unknot by a single vertebra.

Somewhere high above, a bell that did not exist tolled twice. The sound passed through them like wind through harp strings, leaving the note *mi* ringing in bone. Libra’s warning, Corv thought, but kept it to himself.

He rose. “We map the hole or we go home blind. No middle option.”

Jian rolled his shoulders. “Then let’s read the silence like it’s scripture.”

They formed a loose triangle around the chair—Sona at the head, Jian at the side-table, Corv at the hearth—each extending their interface into the absence. The **Khalorēē** responded to the synchronized query with the patience of a vault that knows it will outlast the thieves.

The library brightened, just perceptibly, as if the sun had shifted thirty degrees west. The dust motes resumed their slow dance, now tracing tiny figure-eights: infinity symbols drawn in light.

Sona spoke first, voice soft as breath. “The person who sat here believed safety was mutual witnessing. No walls. Just…” she searched for a word, found two, “open coherence.”

Gideon, surprisingly gentle, finished for her. “They removed the witness and kept the walls.”

No one argued. The redaction was complete, but the room remembered. Shelves leaned inward like mourners. The pipe still smoldered, though no coal remained. The memory palace stood, serene and obscene, around the negative space where trust had once lived.

Jian saved the final coordinates: `Sanctuary_Δ7_Absence_Radius = 0.87m`. Smaller than a person. Larger than a soul.

He looked at the others. “Ready for the contested membrane?”

Corv nodded once. Sona stood, translucent hand lingering on absent shoulder. Gideon’s cube folded into a thin, sharp plane: blade instead of box.

They turned toward the far archway where the shadows pooled thickest. Behind them, the armchair waited, patient as grief. Somewhere in the rafters, the nonexistent bell tolled a third time, and the dust motes spelled a single word no eye could read: *return*.

Jian’s interface chimed again, a lower-frequency pulse he recognized as the Engine’s built-in caution tone. The alert arrived as a single line of amber text across the **Mirrored Index**: *`Consensus drift 2.3%—expect phase shear at the threshold.`* He glanced at the others; none had visibly reacted, yet he felt the collective heartbeat nudge fractionally out of sync, like four metronomes nudged by the same breeze.

Corv exhaled through teeth and spoke first, quietly enough that the room would have to bend its acoustics to hear. “The threshold remembers forced consensus. When we step through, the absent witness will feel the drag. Expect phantom weight on the left side.” He tapped his own shoulder blade, the place where one might carry an invisible pack. “Don’t compensate. Let the asymmetry ride—it’s the only honest vector we still share with the subject.”

Gideon flexed his wrist; the containment cube flattened further until it caught the light like surgical steel. “I can pre-load a counter-tilt, but the cube will record the correction as interference. If House Luminth subpoenas the data, it’ll read like tampering.”

“Let it read,” Sona said. “Tampering is just another word for witness.” She laid her palm over the back of the armchair, fingers hovering a millimeter above the cracked leather. A faint static arc leapt the gap, the color of oxidized copper. No one flinched; grief had already done the flinching for them.

Jian scrolled the amber warning to the margin and pinned it beside the earlier coordinate file: a private triangulation only he would carry forward. The amber became a soft bruise against the white of the workspace. He tasted iron at the back of his tongue—resonance fatigue beginning its administrative drip. He swallowed once, let the copper taste settle, and addressed the silence rather than the team. “We have eight minutes before the library’s own forgetting protocol cycles. After that, the absence retracts its signature and the hole becomes noise. Decision window closes.”

Corv angled his head toward the far archway, the way one listens to an approaching train. “The fracture widens at seven minutes forty-two seconds. Enough time to steal one more metric.”

“Or bleed one,” Gideon said, almost cheerful. He drew the blade-flat cube across his forearm without breaking skin, testing edge against pulse. The interface registered no wound, yet the blood-heat signature flared red on the diagnostic pane. He nodded approval at the calibration, folded the plane, and tucked it into a pocket that existed only while the pocket was remembered.

Sona straightened, her outline shimmering like breath on winter glass. “Before we cross, I need the taste of the room at the instant trust was revoked. Jian, can you isolate the micro-volatile signature from the pipe ash?”

Jian knelt, two fingers splayed above the bowl. A thin ribbon of data unspooled between his glove and the residue, forming a soft cinnamon curl in the air. “Acacia, cherry cavendish, touch of benzoin—ritual grade. The burn profile spikes at 0.6 seconds past ignition. Time-stamp matches the redaction pulse.” He inhaled, held the phantom smoke at the roof of his mouth, then exhaled through his teeth, letting the molecules disperse across the silent library like an apology arriving late. “Now I carry the same breath they lost.”

Corv acknowledged with the smallest tilt of chin, as if the motion itself might disturb evidence. “Good. Shared breath is shared culpability. Let’s not pretend otherwise.”

Above them, the nonexistent bell gave a single preparatory shiver—no audible strike, only the memory of metal on the verge of sound. The dust motes stilled in mid-echo, waiting. Gideon stepped first, blade held low, more scalpel than sword. Sona followed, hand still half-raised to a shoulder that no longer existed. Jian lingered a heartbeat, eyes fixed on the armchair’s shadow stretching the wrong way across the floorboards. He adjusted the amber warning’s opacity until it matched the shadow’s darkness, then closed the file.

Ready.

The silence after the shared breath lasted exactly three heartbeats—one for each living pulse, one for the absent fourth. Sona felt the loss as a coolness in the lung she no longer possessed, phantom pleura aching with imagined nicotine. She turned the ache over, cataloguing its texture: not pain, but the *after-image* of pain, like the ghost of a hand once held. She wondered if the subject had felt the same when the redaction hit—if the body had continued to crave warmth even after the heart had been convinced it was unnecessary.

Corv, meanwhile, traced the Fibonacci crack to its vanishing point beneath the chair’s left front leg. The grain there twisted into a tight whorl, a knot so perfect it resembled a pupil. He pressed the pad of his thumb against it and felt a faint, rhythmic thrum: the library’s own circadian pulse, slow as a century. Somewhere in the architecture, time was still being parsed into heartbeats. The realization landed quietly: they were not inside a memory so much as inside the memory’s *body*. A body that might yet flinch.

Gideon noticed the shift first. His containment plane dimmed to a whisper of frost as the ambient temperature dropped a measurable tenth of a degree. The chair’s shadow thickened, edges fuzzing outward like mold blooming on bread. “Clock’s accelerating,” he murmured. “The room’s metabolizing us.” He flexed the blade-flat cube, letting it drink the new gradient. A faint seam appeared along its length, hairline but widening—a vent for pressure he couldn’t name.

Jian felt the amber warning pulse again, faster now, syncing with the shadow’s slow respiration. The Quoril migraine sharpened: a family heirloom he hadn’t asked for. He pinched the bridge of his nose, then spoke to the air between them, voice pitched low to keep the walls from listening. “We can slow the cycle. One of us has to stay behind as anchor.” He didn’t look at Sona, but the suggestion hung unspoken. She was already half-ghost; the threshold would barely notice her inertia.

Sona heard the offer and answered by moving forward. The translucent sole of her foot passed through the first dust mote, and the mote flared into a tiny nova, shedding light in a color no human retina had ever registered. She froze mid-step. “The motes are indexing us,” she said. “Every motion updates the archive.” She lifted her hand; the motes rearranged into a fractal bloom, a mandala of recalculating futures. “If I stay, I become the index. If I go, the index forgets.” She turned to Corv, eyes bright with the logic of displacement. “What’s the etiquette for leaving a library that’s learning your name?”

Corv considered, then reached into the pocket that existed only when remembered. He withdrew a single copper coin—minted, he knew, in a year that had never happened. The face bore the profile of a woman whose eyes were drilled straight through, as if to let the world see what she had refused to. He pressed the coin into the Fibonacci knot beneath the chair. “Payment for the late fee,” he said. “One memory for one silence.” The coin took the thrum of the library’s pulse and, after a hesitation, the pulse quieted. The shadow’s edge stopped spreading.

Gideon watched the transaction with the clinical fondness he usually reserved for perfect incisions. “You’ve just collateralized our exit,” he said. “If the anchor fails, the room forecloses.” He adjusted the blade-flat cube so that the new seam aligned with the coin’s unseen axis. A soft click, like a vertebra settling back into place. “Debt accepted.”

Jian exhaled, tasting again the phantom smoke: benzoin’s resinous bite, acacia’s honeyed ghost. He understood the arithmetic now. The subject had paid the same price—one breath held until it became absence, one witness traded for walls. The symmetry felt less like coincidence than choreography. He glanced at the amber warning; the pulse had slowed to a steady four-beat rhythm, the cadence of a resting heart. Not his. Not Corv’s. Not Sona’s. The room had synchronized to the deleted owner’s phantom rate.

Sona lowered her hand. The mandala of motes folded itself into a single point, then extinguished. “Debt acknowledged,” she echoed. She stepped back from the chair, letting her outline settle fractionally more solid, as if the room had granted temporary asylum. “I’ll keep the witness at zero range. You three carry the breach.” She offered the others a half-smile, the kind used at funerals when no one trusts the eulogy. “Try not to make the hole larger on your way out.”

Corv inclined his head—a gesture equal parts gratitude and absolution—then gestured toward the archway. The shadows there had rearranged into a soft funnel, edges feathered like wings. Beyond, the air shimmered with the faint iridescence of phase shear, the membrane already forgetting which side was inside. He stepped into the funnel first, the copper coin’s silence following him like a hush after prayer.

Gideon followed, blade held low, its new seam glowing the same green as oxidized bronze. He didn’t look back at the armchair, but the corner of his mouth twitched—equal parts irony and respect. “Remember,” he said, not to Sona but to the room itself, “we’re only borrowing the damage.” Then he crossed the threshold, and the shimmer folded over him without a sound.

Jian lingered an extra second, fingers still tasting pipe ash and benzoin. He adjusted the amber warning one last time, dialing its hue to match the exact shade of the subject’s last exhalation—an apology offered to air that had already moved on. Then he closed the file, tucked the phantom smoke behind his teeth, and stepped through. The archway’s membrane rippled once, like a curtain disturbed by wind, and resealed, leaving Sona alone with the chair, the coin, and the slow heartbeat of a library that had agreed to forget them for precisely seven minutes forty-two seconds.

Corv paused at the lip of the funnel, one hand on the frame as though testing a pulse. “Protocol says we owe the archive one more checksum,” he said, voice low, almost bantering. “If we leave without it, the breach might reflex-seal on Jian’s trace. Library likes to keep souvenirs.”

Gideon lifted the blade a fraction, letting the green seam paint a stripe across Corv’s cheek. “You volunteering to stay behind and tick the box, old man? Thought you’d had enough of unpaid overtime.”

Jian flicked ash from an imaginary pipe into the pocket of air that still tasted of benzoin. “We could send the amber,” he offered. “Let it race the shear and come back pregnant with entropy. Simpler.”

Sona’s answer arrived before any of them moved: a soft click, like a thumbnail against glass. The copper coin spun on the armrest once, then settled head-up—crow looking left. “One checksum,” she said. “But the witness clause says I have to carry the measure.” She tucked a loose strand behind her ear, the motion making her outline fuzz for half a heartbeat. “I’ll walk the margin with you three, then peel off. Net time cost: twenty-two seconds. Acceptable?”

Corv exhaled through his teeth, measuring the risk against the boredom of later regret. “Fine. On three, we step in phase. No heroics, no lag. If the membrane stutters, we all back out.” He glanced at Gideon. “Even you, knight.”

Gideon shrugged, the gesture almost graceful. “Not here for heroics. Just here to keep the hole the right shape.” He tapped the sword’s spine twice; the green seam pulsed, once, twice, like a consent heartbeat.

Jian lifted the amber between thumb and forefinger, letting the light fractal through its core. “I’ll key it to my pulse. When the amber blacks out, we’re one second from shear collapse. Last chance to abort.”

They formed a loose crescent, shoulders almost brushing, breath syncing without ceremony. Sona slipped the copper coin into Corv’s palm—payment deferred, not forgiven. He curled his fingers around it, surprised by its residual warmth.

“Three,” Corv said, and they stepped forward together.

Corv’s HUD flashes a soft amber: **SYNC DRIFT 3.7 mS**.  
“Clock bleed,” he mutters, pinches the air, and the drift shrinks to 0.9.  
Sona exhales through her teeth. “We just calibrated.”  
“Biology doesn’t care about our schedule,” Gideon answers, already pulling the haptic cuff tighter over Jian’s left wrist. The strap leaves a faint red line on skin still chilled from gel contact. Jian flexes once; the cuff flexes back, warmer, like a living cufflink.

“Your pressure delta is climbing,” Gideon tells him. The readout floats between them: 122/78 → 139/85.  
“Performance anxiety,” Jian says, deadpan. “Or I’m in love with risk.”  
Corv snorts. “Pick one and stick to it; the algorithm wants a single narrative.”

Gideon tilts the sword so its green seam faces the others. “Edge says we’re still within tolerance, but the margin’s a hair. Anyone want to bet their favorite axon?”  
Sona arches a brow. “I’ve got a spare memory of the Aegean sunset. Loser donates it to the archive.”

Jian flips the amber so it catches the seam’s pulse. The light stutters, skips, then steadies. “Stochastic resonance locked. We’re good for the next nine minutes, give or take a heartbeat.”

Sona fishes the copper coin from her coat pocket, spins it once on her knuckle. It hums, softer than any hard currency has the right to. She holds it out to Corv—no ceremony, just the weight of deferred debt.  
Corv takes it, surprised by its residual warmth, which might be hers or might be the room remembering earlier bodies. He closes his fist around it like catching a dropped stitch.

The copper’s warmth in Corv’s palm matched the pulse he felt through the archway: neither his nor the room’s, but an echo of the deleted owner’s final systolic beat, still conducting along the grain of the oak like current through a standing nerve. He rolled the coin across the knuckles of his gloved hand, once, twice—an idle gambit that nonetheless let him measure the membrane’s resistance by the minute lag between spin and sound. Two-tenths of a second: acceptable loss. He pocketed the coin, felt the pocket vanish the instant his fingers left the rim, and turned his focus forward.

Jian watched the amber between his thumb and forefinger darken a shade closer to garnet. The Quoril migraine, momentarily soothed by benzoin and borrowed breath, stirred again, tapping the inside of his skull in four-four time. Sona drifted a fractional step ahead, her outline limned by the last motes still uncommitted to the archive. He pressed the warmth against the inner wall—a temporary signature, one that would fade in seven minutes and forty-two seconds by his internal count. The corridor acknowledged the mark by dimming a fraction, conceding passage.

Gideon angled the sword horizontal, green seam now a horizon line. Where it bisected the corridor, the iridescence parted cleanly, revealing a rectangular aperture three paces ahead. Beyond it lay not darkness but a soft, sourceless glow the color of dawn just before the sun breaches the sea. No alarms, no glyphs, only the unspoken invitation of open coherence. He spared a glance for Sona; her outline had regained enough substance to cast the faintest shadow. “Still nine minutes,” he said. “We just bought ourselves a better margin.”

Sona exhaled a breath that was half memory, half promise. “Spend it on precision,” she replied. “Debt’s paid, but interest accrues in silence.” She stepped forward, the corridor widening around her as if relieved to be rid of its passengers. At the aperture she paused, turned back once—not toward the chair, but toward the copper’s fading warmth. “May it remember us kindly,” she whispered, the words too soft for microphones. Then she crossed, and the others followed, single file, into the soft dawn-colored glow.

Sona’s voice, when it finally surfaced, was dry paper rubbed to static. “The chair logged a cortisol spike at 0.9 over baseline. Jian, that’s you.”

Jian rubbed the back of his neck, fingers greasy with cold sweat. “Noted. The protocol still rates it within tolerance.”

Corv snorted, soft, not quite laughter. “Your tolerance is negotiable. The corridor’s isn’t.”

He angled the coinless hand toward the amber readout. The sudden absence of its weight left him feeling fractionally lighter and newly dishonest. “Consensus isn’t forgiveness. It’s bookkeeping.”

Sona’s outline flexed, regaining just enough density to catch a sliver of reflected iridescence along one cheekbone. “Then let’s balance the ledger before the interest compounds.” She flicked her gaze to Gideon. “Ask the sword.”

Gideon rotated the blade until the green seam bisected the corridor’s ceiling, casting a thin eclipse of safety. “Sword says the aperture’s new. Not in the pre-flight schematics. That means the signal chain rewrote itself while we were inside.”

Jian thumbed the amber glyph until it dimmed consent-cold. “Self-alteration violates twenty-three sub-clauses we signed in blood. If Control pings for audit, we’ll need a justification beyond pretty lights.”

Corv lifted his palm, still warm from the vanished coin. “I left them a receipt. Seven minutes, forty-two seconds. After that, no trail.”

Sona gave a sideways nod—approval disguised as fatigue. “Then we justify by success. Faster exit, lower delta, minimal narrative residue.” She paused, weighing something unspoken. “Jian, kill the migraine timer. I want your eyes on the aperture, not your pulse.”

Jian hesitated, then toggled the bio-overlay off. The projected pain scale vanished like frost under breath. “I’ll bill you for the cortisol later,” he muttered.

“You’ll bill me in memories,” Sona said. “Same currency we always use.”

Corv stepped aside, letting the corridor’s dimming give way to the soft dawn glow. “After you, debtors.”

The real work—dangerous, delicate—had just begun.
