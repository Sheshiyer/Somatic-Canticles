Sona flicked the dead candidate’s chronograph from her wrist, felt the clasp bite skin before she let it drop into Corv’s cupped palm. The watch still ticked, but the data stream had frozen at the instant the Klei lattice ruptured—three missed heartbeats and a spike of cortisol the size of a small panic attack.  

“Time of death: seventeen seconds ago,” Corv said, voice low enough that only the four living pulses could hear. He turned the bezel until the second hand snapped backward one tick, then forward again—an apology to physics.  

Gideon watched without comment, but his thumb brushed the cracked journal’s spine as though asking its paper bones whether seventeen seconds was enough to write an epitaph. The Klei root pulsed once, retracted another centimeter, then stilled like a snake remembering the room still had corners.  

Jian adjusted the bearing on his retinal HUD, frowned at the 0.3-degree drift that hadn’t been there five minutes earlier. “Field resonance is tightening. We’re moving from guide to guest list.”  

Sona arched an eyebrow. “Explain the etiquette.”  

“If the lattice decides we’re hosts, we carry the party favors. If it decides we’re favors, we stay on the plate.”  

Corv snorted. “Party favors bleed less if we keep the rhythm polite.” He tapped the journal once, a hollow wooden knock that carried more weight than the thing deserved. “Dead man’s last HRV trace is still in here. We could scrub it, or we could let it ride shotgun.”  

Gideon’s grin arrived sideways. “You volunteering as chaperone for a panic attack?”  

“Somebody has to keep it from ordering another round.”  

Sona stepped closer, angled her wrist so the faint green glow of her own pulse overlapped Corv’s. The two lattices—one living, one fossilized—shimmered like overlapping transparencies. She tasted iron at the back of her throat, same flavor that had coated her mouth when the failed candidate’s heart stuttered into silence. “Consent remains mutual until proven otherwise,” she said quietly. “We vote.”  

Jian closed the bearing adjustment tab, felt the soft click travel down his spine like vertebra remembering alignment. “All in favor of incorporating the trace.”  

Three fingers lifted in the half-light: Corv’s, Gideon’s, Sona’s. The air warmed by a single degree—approval, or indigestion.  

Corv opened the journal to the last recorded page. The ink had spread, forming a branched pattern too symmetrical for capillary action alone. He pressed the Klei root against the paper. Grey filaments crawled outward, knitting the ink back into letters that hadn’t existed when the candidate died. The new script read: *I was afraid of the wrong story. Leave the fear, take the plot.*  

Gideon exhaled through his teeth, the sound half whistle, half sigh. “He just ghost-edited his own obituary. Efficient.”  

Sona felt her pulse sync with the dead man’s last cadence—an arrhythmic hiccup that lasted three beats before her body overruled it. The merge registered on Jian’s HUD as a soft amber ripple across both metrics: *Coherence 78%.*  

“That’s within tolerance,” Jian said. “But we’ll need to walk it off.”  

They rearranged formation without spoken cue: Sona on point, Jian bearing compass, Corv and Gideon bracketing the journal like honor guard. Their boots found the resin floor’s pre-worn scallops—each impression an invitation to land lighter than the last.  

The corridor hummed at twenty-eight hertz now, two cycles lower, like a lullaby reconsidering its major key. Sona tasted cardamom again, then a sudden bright note of citrus rind, followed by the copper tang of blood not hers. She swallowed twice to keep it from becoming story; some things were seasoning, not sustenance.  

Halfway to the fork, Jian halted, eyes unfocused on a memory only he could parse. “Field’s offering a detour. Left corridor shows ice. Right shows city. Both are memories, not geography.”  

Corv lifted the journal, angled it so the new ink caught the corridor’s ambient glow. “He was born in a cold port city. Ice on the docks, diesel in the lungs. Could be he wants us to pick his childhood.”  

Sona flexed her wrist, felt the dead man’s pulse ghost-beat against her own. “Or he’s testing whether we’ll confuse nostalgia with navigation.”  

Gideon traced a fingertip along the Klei root’s retracted coils, felt the subtle vibration that meant the lattice was taking notes. “We vote again?”  

“No,” Sona said. “We walk until the corridor decides for us.”  

They moved. The hum dropped another cycle; walls bled frost along the left seam while the right exhaled humid asphalt. Each footfall stirred micro-eddies of temperature that licked at their cheeks like indecisive weather.  

Corv broke the silence first, voice pitched for Sona’s left ear alone. “You ever carry a mortality you didn’t earn?”  

She considered, letting the question settle under her tongue. “Only every time I finish someone else’s sentence.”  

He laughed once, a dry click that might have been a cough. “Then we’re in good company.”  

Ahead, the fork resolved into two identical doorways—no frames, just thresholds breathing opposite climates. Frost whispered from the left; warm rain hissed from the right. The corridor itself hovered at equilibrium, a zero-sum breath between seasons.  

Jian’s HUD flashed soft gold: *Consensus window closing in sixty seconds.* He turned to the others, expression unreadable under helmet glass. “System’s asking for a named destination. We can give it a city, a dock, or a heart rate.”  

Gideon tapped the journal against his sternum, felt the candidate’s final cadence flutter like a moth against ribcage. “We give it all three and let the lattice average the ache.”  

Corv raised an eyebrow. “Poetic, but the math’s brutal.”  

“Math always is,” Gideon replied. “That’s why we hired poetry.”  

Sona stepped between the two thresholds, arms loose at her sides. Frost kissed her left cheek; warm rain freckled the right. She inhaled both, held the hybrid until her lungs felt like bilingual instruments, then spoke the dead man’s last words aloud: “Leave the fear, take the plot.”  

The corridor exhaled once, a long metallic sigh that tasted of wet iron. Frost and rain collapsed toward a single point between the doorways, forming a bead of condensation no larger than a tear. It hovered, trembled, then shot forward along the resin floor, sketching a single scalloped line that angled neither left nor right but precisely along the bearing Jian had adjusted: 0.3 degrees off true.  

Jian watched the bead vanish into the narrowing distance, then nodded once. “Field accepts the amendment. We follow the line.”  

Corv snapped the chronograph closed and slid it into a thigh pocket, metal cool against his skin. “Time of amended departure: now.”  

They walked. The corridor narrowed enough that shoulders brushed both walls, exchanging static sparks at every heartbeat. Sona tasted the cardamom note again, followed by a softer echo of diesel—someone else’s childhood arriving as aftershave. She let the flavors pass without comment.  

Behind them, the two doorways sealed with simultaneous clicks, like parentheses closing around a phrase no longer needed. The hum stabilized at twenty-seven hertz, the corridor’s way of saying *settled.*  

Gideon adjusted the journal so the spine pressed against the rib he’d cracked years ago, felt the small shock of recognition: old pain meeting borrowed heartbeat. He flexed the Klei root once more; it retracted fully, leaving only a faint ring of heat around his wrist.  

No one spoke. The silence held its own coherence, a shared lungful of direction without ownership.
